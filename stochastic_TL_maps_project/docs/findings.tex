\section*{Executive Summary}

\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Executive Summary}

This report evaluates four uncertainty quantification (UQ) methods for
underwater acoustic transmission loss (TL) computed by the Bellhop ray-tracing
model.
The uncertain inputs are carrier frequency, source depth ($z_S$), and sound
velocity profile (SVP), each modelled as an independent random variable.
Three analytical methods --- the 2\textsuperscript{nd}-order Delta method,
Polynomial Chaos Expansion (PCE), and a 3-parameter lognormal (LN3) fit --- are
compared against Monte Carlo (MC) ground truth across four propagation scenarios
(shallow water, deep water, upslope, downslope) and three input
uncertainty distributions (Normal\,1/5/10\%).

\textbf{Key conclusions:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item The \textbf{Delta method} (1\textsuperscript{st}-order Taylor) is
        reliable for flat bathymetry and small perturbations ($\leq$1\%), but the
        2\textsuperscript{nd}-order Hessian correction already exceeds 96\% of the
        total variance at 5\% uncertainty, indicating the Taylor series has not
        converged.  Delta fails at slope bathymetry regardless of perturbation
        level.
  \item \textbf{PCE} (fitted to the existing $N=1000$ MC samples at zero extra
        Bellhop cost) requires LOO-CV-selected order $K^* = 5$--$12$ across all
        scenario/distribution combinations, reflecting moderate-to-strong nonlinearity
        throughout.  The corrected pipeline (non-zero PCE coefficients) shows all
        parameters require $K^*\geq 5$; the previously reported $K^*\leq 3$ were
        artifacts of a coefficient bug.  Only upslope/Normal\,10\%/freq ($K^*=12$)
        approaches the $K_{\max}=20$ limit; all other cases converge within 5--9 terms.
        Slope scenarios require full MC for large perturbations.
  \item The \textbf{Delta--LN3 hybrid} (moment-matched shadow probability)
        performs well for flat scenarios but degrades at shadow-zone boundaries in
        slope environments.
  \item \textbf{Full Monte Carlo} is unavoidable for slope bathymetry ($\geq$5\%
        perturbation) and for $z_S$ in deep water with Normal distributions.
  \item The \textbf{LN3 distribution fit} uses MLE via \texttt{lognfit} for the
        shifted parameters, retaining a heuristic shift $\gamma = 0.95\min$:
        full 3-parameter MLE is computationally prohibitive at 440\,000 pixels.
        A design-weighted likelihood or survey-sampling correction would be needed for a
        rigorous MLE fit; this remains an open methodological limitation.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Practical recommendation:} For sonar mission planning, use
PCE-$K^*$ (order 5--9) on flat-bottom scenarios and fall back to MC for slope
environments or large source-depth uncertainty.  Report $P_\mathrm{fail}$ and
P95 TL maps rather than variance alone, as variance does not distinguish the
\emph{direction} of acoustic uncertainty.

\section{Understanding the Problem}
\label{sec:understanding}

This section establishes the physical and mathematical foundation of the
stochastic TL mapping problem.
The goal is to explain why a single deterministic Bellhop TL map is
insufficient for operational sonar planning, and to provide the theoretical
background behind the UQ methods compared in the rest of this report.

% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{The Ocean as a Layered Acoustic Medium}
\label{sec:ocean_layers}

Sound travels through seawater at approximately 1400--1550\,m/s, a speed
governed by temperature, pressure, and salinity.
None of these three is uniform with depth.
Temperature drops sharply through the \emph{thermocline} before stabilising in
the deep isothermal layer, while hydrostatic pressure increases monotonically.
Their competing effects produce a characteristic sound-velocity profile (SVP)
with a minimum near the thermocline base --- the \emph{sound-channel axis} ---
that refracts acoustic energy downward above the axis and upward below it,
trapping low-frequency sound in a natural waveguide.

\subsection{Acoustic Wave Propagation and the Bellhop Forward Model}
\label{sec:wave_eq}

\subsubsection{The Wave Equation}

The pressure field $p(r,z,t)$ radiated by a harmonic point source of frequency
$f$ at depth $z_s$ in an axisymmetric medium satisfies:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial^2 p}{\partial r^2}
  + \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial p}{\partial r}
  + \frac{\partial^2 p}{\partial z^2}
  + \frac{\omega^2}{c^2(z)}\,p
  \;=\; -\frac{\delta(r)}{r}\,\delta(z - z_s),
  \label{eq:helmholtz}
\end{equation}
where $r$ is horizontal range, $z$ is depth (positive downward),
$\omega = 2\pi f$, and $c(z)$ is the depth-dependent sound speed
(the SVP).
Equation~\eqref{eq:helmholtz} is the frequency-domain (Helmholtz) form of
the wave equation in cylindrical coordinates; it is the governing PDE that
all acoustic propagation models --- including Bellhop --- solve.

\subsubsection{Transmission Loss}

The \textbf{transmission loss} (TL) at a receiver at $(r,z)$ is:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{TL}(r,z) \;=\; -20\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{|p(r,z)|}{|p_0|}\right)
  \quad [\mathrm{dB}],
  \label{eq:TL_def}
\end{equation}
where $|p_0|$ is the pressure amplitude at 1\,m reference distance.
TL increases with range as acoustic energy spreads and is absorbed.
Focussing effects (convergence zones) temporarily reduce TL.

\subsubsection{Bellhop as a Black-Box Forward Model}

\textbf{Bellhop} is a ray-tracing solver for Eq.~\eqref{eq:helmholtz}.
Given environmental inputs --- SVP, bathymetry, frequency $f$, source depth
$z_S$ --- it traces a fan of rays through the medium and computes a full 2-D
TL map $\mathrm{TL}(r,z)$.
It is treated in this project as a \emph{black-box forward model}: for each
parameter sample $\boldsymbol\omega$, Bellhop is called once and returns a
TL cube; no gradient or Jacobian information is extracted directly from the
solver.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.82\textwidth]{pptx_figures/image7.png}
  \caption{Representative Bellhop TL map: deep-water flat bathymetry with a
           summer SVP, $f=10{,}000$\,Hz, $z_S = 5$\,m.
           TL (dB, colour scale) increases with range as energy spreads.

\end{figure}

% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{The Sonar Equation and the Figure of Merit}
\label{sec:sonar_eq}

Detection of an underwater target is governed by the \emph{active sonar
equation}.
Expressing all quantities in dB:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{SNR}
  \;=\;
  \underbrace{\mathrm{SL} - \mathrm{TL} + \mathrm{TS}}_{\text{received signal level}}
  \;-\;
  \underbrace{(\mathrm{NL} - \mathrm{DI})}_{\text{effective noise level}}
  \;\geq\; \mathrm{DT},
  \label{eq:sonar_eq}
\end{equation}
where SL is the source level, TS is target strength, NL is ambient noise
level, DI is the array's directivity index, and DT is the detection threshold.

Rearranging the detection condition gives:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{TL}(r,z) \;\leq\;
  \underbrace{\mathrm{SL} + \mathrm{TS} - \mathrm{NL} + \mathrm{DI} - \mathrm{DT}}_{\displaystyle\mathrm{FOM}},
  \label{eq:fom}
\end{equation}
where \textbf{FOM} (Figure of Merit) is determined entirely by the sonar
system and target, independent of the environment.
A target at $(r,z)$ is detectable if and only if $\mathrm{TL}(r,z) \leq \mathrm{FOM}$.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Why TL is the right quantity to model.}
SL, TS, NL, DI, and DT are platform- and system-dependent constants.
TL is driven purely by ocean physics.
A probabilistic TL map --- $P(\mathrm{TL}(r,z;\boldsymbol\omega) > \mathrm{FOM})$
--- is therefore the universal deliverable: it quantifies detection risk as a
function of position for \emph{any} sonar whose FOM is known, without requiring
knowledge of the specific system architecture.

% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Propagation Phenomena: Convergence Zones and Sea-Floor Reflections}
\label{sec:propagation_phenomena}

Two propagation mechanisms dominate the TL field in the scenarios studied:

\paragraph{Convergence zones.}
Rays launched downward by a shallow source are curved upward by the
depth-increasing pressure gradient below the thermocline.
For typical open-ocean SVPs they re-focus at the surface at ranges of
40--60\,km (and at multiples thereof), creating arcs of anomalously low TL.
CZ position, width, and gain are all sensitive to the SVP gradient profile.

\paragraph{Sea-floor reflections.}
In shallow water or slope bathymetry, downward rays encounter the seabed and
reflect back, creating additional interfering paths.
The reflected TL map has a qualitatively different character: monotonically
increasing TL without the CZ focussing arcs present in the deep-water case.

% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{The Stochastic Extension: Why Uncertainty Matters}
\label{sec:stochastic_motivation}

Deterministic Bellhop models treat the three environmental inputs --- carrier
frequency $f$, source depth $z_S$, and SVP --- as exactly known.
In practice, all three carry uncertainty:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Frequency ($f$):} Oscillator drift, Doppler shift from platform
        motion, and signal bandwidth all introduce frequency uncertainty.
  \item \textbf{Source depth ($z_S$):} The adversary's depth is unknown to the
        receiver; own-platform depth varies with sea state and cable catenary for
        towed arrays.
  \item \textbf{SVP:} Temperature, salinity, and pressure vary continuously in
        space and time.
        The SVP is measured at discrete locations (bathythermograph drops) and
        interpolated; internal waves and mesoscale fronts introduce additional
        structure below the measurement resolution.
\end{itemize}

We model each parameter as an independent scalar random variable:
\begin{equation}
  \boldsymbol{\omega}
  = \bigl(\omega_f,\;\omega_{z_S},\;\omega_\mathrm{svp}\bigr)
  \;\sim\; p_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol\omega),
  \label{eq:stochastic_params}
\end{equation}
with marginal distributions specified as Normal or Uniform with standard
deviations set to 1\%, 5\%, or 10\% of the respective nominal value.
This renders the TL field stochastic:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{TL}(r,z;\,\boldsymbol\omega)
  \;\longrightarrow\;
  \begin{cases}
    \mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathrm{TL}(r,z)\right] & \text{mean TL map}\\[3pt]
    \mathrm{Var}\!\left[\mathrm{TL}(r,z)\right] & \text{variance map}\\[3pt]
    P\!\left(\mathrm{TL}(r,z;\boldsymbol\omega) > \mathrm{FOM}\right)
      & \text{detection-failure probability map}
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:uq_goal}
\end{equation}
Computing all three outputs at every range--depth pixel, across
$4\;\text{scenarios}\times3\;\text{distributions}=12$ combinations,
efficiently and without redundant Bellhop runs is the central engineering
challenge of this project.

\subsection{Analytical Validation of Bellhop}
\label{sec:validation}

Before using Bellhop as the stochastic forward model, we verified it against
two analytical closed-form solutions of Eq.~\eqref{eq:helmholtz}.

\subsubsection{Point Source in a Fluid Half-Space (Method of Images)}

For a point source at depth $z_s$ in a homogeneous fluid bounded above by a
pressure-release surface ($p|_{z=0}=0$), the exact Green's function is
obtained by adding an anti-source (image source) at $z=-z_s$:
\begin{equation}
  p(r,z) = \frac{e^{ik r_1}}{r_1} - \frac{e^{ik r_2}}{r_2},
  \qquad
  r_{1,2} = \sqrt{r^2 + (z\mp z_s)^2},
  \label{eq:method_images}
\end{equation}
where $k = \omega/c$ is the acoustic wavenumber.
The two terms produce constructive and destructive interference, creating a
striped TL pattern with fringe spacing $\Delta r \approx \lambda\,r/(2z_s)$.

    \caption{Analytical solution via method of images
             (Eq.~\ref{eq:method_images}): $f=1{,}000$\,Hz, $z_S=5$\,m.
             Fine interference fringes from superposition of direct and

    \label{fig:analytical_half_space}
  \end{subfigure}\hfill

    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{pptx_figures/image36.png}
    \caption{Bellhop output for the identical setup.
             The fringe spacing, orientation, and amplitude closely match

             implementation.}
    \label{fig:bellhop_half_space}
  \end{subfigure}
  \caption{Validation of Bellhop against the analytical half-space solution.
           Agreement in fringe structure confirms that Bellhop correctly
           implements wave interference and geometric spreading for this
           geometry.}
  \label{fig:validation_half_space}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Point Source in a Waveguide (Normal-Mode Solution)}

When the water column is bounded above ($z=0$, pressure-release) and below
($z=H$, rigid or impedance-matched seabed), the pressure field decomposes into
a discrete set of \emph{normal modes}:
\begin{equation}
  p(r,z) = \frac{i}{4\rho}
  \sum_{n=1}^{N_\mathrm{modes}}
  \psi_n(z_s)\,\psi_n(z)\,H_0^{(1)}(k_n r),
  \label{eq:normal_modes}
\end{equation}
where the depth eigenfunctions $\psi_n(z)$ satisfy
$\psi_n'' + [k^2(z)-k_n^2]\,\psi_n = 0$ with the boundary conditions,
$H_0^{(1)}$ is the Hankel function, and $k_n$ is the horizontal wavenumber
of mode $n$.
Mode $n$ propagates only above its \emph{cut-off frequency}
$f_n^c = n\,c/(2H)$; below cut-off, the mode is evanescent.
The superposition of multiple modes with different $k_n$ produces the complex
interference pattern seen in real ocean TL maps.

\subsection{Sensitivity of TL to Physical Parameters}
\label{sec:param_sensitivity}

To motivate the three-parameter stochastic model, we briefly demonstrate how
TL changes qualitatively when each parameter is varied in isolation.
These are the same parameter perturbations that the UQ methods must characterise
statistically.

\subsubsection{Sound Velocity Profile (SVP)}

The SVP controls ray curvature.
A higher sound speed produces wider refraction angles, tighter CZ spacing, and
a qualitatively different TL map.

  \centering
  \begin{subfigure}[t]{0.32\textwidth}
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{pptx_figures/image17.png}
    \caption{Low SVP: minimal refraction, nearly spherical geometric
             spreading, no convergence zone.}
  \end{subfigure}\hfill
  \begin{subfigure}[t]{0.32\textwidth}
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{pptx_figures/image22.png}
    \caption{Moderate SVP: partial refraction, faint near-field focusing
             structure beginning to appear.}
  \end{subfigure}\hfill
  \begin{subfigure}[t]{0.32\textwidth}
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{pptx_figures/image30.png}
    \caption{Higher SVP: strong refraction, pronounced convergence-zone
             arc at short range.}
  \end{subfigure}
  \caption{Effect of SVP on the Bellhop TL map.
           Even modest changes in sound speed qualitatively reorganise the
           propagation structure --- motivating the large and spatially
           heterogeneous variance attributed to SVP perturbation in
           Section~\ref{sec:variance_fingerprint}.}
  \label{fig:svp_sensitivity}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Carrier Frequency}

Acoustic frequency controls wavelength ($\lambda = c/f$) and therefore the
spatial scale of interference fringes.
Higher frequency produces finer fringes.
Crucially, a frequency change shifts all fringes \emph{coherently} across the
entire range--depth plane --- it is a global phase advance, not a structural
reorganisation.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \begin{subfigure}[t]{0.48\textwidth}
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{pptx_figures/image33.png}
    \caption{$f = 2{,}000$\,Hz: fine interference fringes with short fringe
             spacing $\lambda/2$.}
  \end{subfigure}\hfill
  \begin{subfigure}[t]{0.48\textwidth}
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{pptx_figures/image32.png}
    \caption{$f = 1{,}000$\,Hz: fringe spacing doubles relative to left panel;
             the spatial pattern shifts globally but its character is
             preserved.}
  \end{subfigure}
  \caption{Frequency dispersion: halving frequency doubles fringe spacing.
           This coherent global shift is why frequency-induced TL variance is
           spatially \emph{uniform} across all pixels in slope environments
           (Section~\ref{sec:variance_fingerprint}),
           in stark contrast to the heterogeneous variance produced by SVP.}
  \label{fig:freq_sensitivity}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Water-Column Depth and Sea-Floor Distance}

Water-column depth (equivalently, the depth of a receiving sensor relative
to the sea floor) controls how many normal modes propagate and how strongly
sea-floor reflections interact with the direct path.

  \begin{subfigure}[t]{0.32\textwidth}

\subsubsection{Wavelength Regime}

When the acoustic wavelength $\lambda = c/f$ is much smaller than the
waveguide depth $H$, many modes propagate and the TL map shows rich
interference structure (as in Figures~\ref{fig:freq_sensitivity}
and~\ref{fig:depth_sensitivity}).
As $\lambda \to H/n$, only $n$ modes survive and the pattern simplifies
dramatically.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.60\textwidth]{pptx_figures/image24.png}

           ($\lambda \approx 1.5$\,m, water depth\,=\,1.5\,km, giving
           $H/\lambda \approx 1000$ propagating modes).
           At sufficiently low frequency, this complex pattern collapses
           to a simple single-mode structure.
           Bellhop remains accurate across all wavelength regimes
           validated here, from the geometric-optics limit to the
           near-modal limit.}
  \label{fig:standing_waves}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Monte Carlo Reference Statistics}
\label{sec:mc}

Monte Carlo (MC) simulations use \textbf{two complementary sample sets}, each with $N=1000$
samples per scenario/distribution/parameter combination:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{LHS set} ($N=1000$ Latin Hypercube samples): provides
        $\hat\EX[\TL]$, $\hat\Var[\TL]$, empirical $\hat P_{\text{det}}$, KDE,
        and the MOM-fitted LN3 reference.
        LHS stratification ensures full coverage of the input space even at moderate~$N$.
  \item \textbf{IID set} ($N=1000$ independent, identically distributed samples):
        used exclusively for MLE-based LN3 fitting (\texttt{lognfit}),

and \texttt{Cache/<scenario>/<dist>\_iid/} (IID), yielding
$4\times3\times3\times2\times1000 = 72\,000$ total Bellhop evaluations.
All analytical methods (Delta, PCE, LN3) operate on these cached cubes at zero
additional Bellhop cost.
The LHS and IID sets together provide the reference statistics $\EX[\TL]$ and $\Var[\TL]$

\subsubsection{Input Sound-Velocity Profiles and Perturbation Model}
\label{sec:svp_ensemble}

\paragraph{Physical motivation for the SVP perturbation model.}
The ocean sound-speed profile (SVP) is not uniform in depth, and its uncertainty is
not uniform either.
The near-surface mixed layer (0--100\,m in summer, 50--300\,m in winter) is actively
stirred by wind, solar heating, and convective cooling; it is nearly isothermal within
the mixing depth but its temperature and depth vary significantly with season, weather,
and geographic location.
Below the thermocline (the layer of rapid temperature decrease separating the mixed layer
from the deep ocean), water temperature falls to $\approx 1$--$4\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$
and remains essentially constant year-round because solar heating and wind mixing do not
penetrate there.
The deep isothermal layer therefore contributes negligible SVP variability compared to
the surface.

This asymmetry justifies the one-dimensional SVP perturbation model used in this study:
a scalar shift $\Delta c$ applied only to the surface mixed layer,
coupled to a mixed-layer depth (MLD) perturbation
$\Delta\mathrm{MLD} = k_{\mathrm{MLD}}\,\Delta c$
($k_{\mathrm{MLD}} = 5\,\mathrm{m\,s}/\mathrm{m}$, $\mathrm{MLD}_0 = 30\,\mathrm{m}$).
The deep SVP gradient ($z > 180\,\mathrm{m}$) is held constant because:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Deep-water temperature variability is an order of magnitude smaller than
        mixed-layer variability on seasonal time scales.
  \item The dominant sonar performance uncertainty arises from surface-layer SVP,
        which controls refraction of the direct path and the first surface-reflected
        path (both of which pass through the mixed layer for a near-surface source/receiver).
  \item A one-parameter model for the mixed-layer sound speed is the standard assumption
        in operational oceanographic uncertainty analysis (c.f.\ JMSE-10-01548).
\end{enumerate}
This perturbation model is therefore physically analogous to the \emph{seasonal uncertainty
in surface temperature} (summer/winter SST variability of $\pm$1--5\,$^\circ\mathrm{C}$,
which translates to roughly $\pm$5--25\,m/s in surface sound speed via the Mackenzie
formula $\partial c/\partial T \approx 4$\,m/s per $^\circ$C).

\paragraph{Ensemble visualisation.}
Figure~\ref{fig:svp_ensemble} shows the $N=1000$ sampled SVP realisations for the
deep-water scenario under Normal\,5\% and Normal\,10\% uncertainty.
Each blue trace is one MC draw; the bold black curve is the nominal profile.

\subsubsection{MC Estimator Convergence}
\label{sec:mc_conv}

For a scalar random variable $Y$ with finite variance, the sample mean
$\bar Y_N$ converges to $\mu_Y$ at the classical CLT rate
$\mathrm{RMSE}(\bar Y_N) = \sigma_Y / \sqrt{N}$.
The sample variance $S_N^2$ converges at the same asymptotic rate but with a
larger prefactor: for a Gaussian parent,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{Var}(S_N^2) \;=\; \frac{2\sigma_Y^4}{N-1},
  \label{eq:var_of_var}
\end{equation}
so the relative error of $S_N^2$ is $\sqrt{2/(N-1)}\,/\,C_V^2$, where
$C_V = \sigma_Y/\mu_Y$ is the coefficient of variation.
In practice this means that a variance estimate requires roughly $2\times$ as
many samples as a mean estimate to reach the same relative error -- or
equivalently $\approx 4\times$ as many samples to match the absolute RMSE of
the mean.
The pipeline draws $N=1000$ Latin Hypercube samples (LHS) for each
(scenario~$\times$~distribution) combination separately.
LHS stratifies each parameter's marginal CDF into $N$ equal-probability bins and
draws one sample per bin, improving spatial coverage over simple random sampling
but breaking statistical independence between samples (see Section~\ref{sec:ln3}
for the implication on LN3 fitting).
The full MC set consists of $4\,\text{scenarios}\times3\,\text{distributions}\times3\,\text{parameters}
= 36$ separate LHS runs of $N=1000$ Bellhop evaluations each.
At $N=1000$ and for the broadest distribution (Normal\,10\%), the relative RMSE of
the sample variance estimator is
$\sqrt{2/(N-1)}/C_V^2 \approx 4.5\%$, well within the $\lesssim 5\%$ target for
the high-uncertainty cases, and lower still for narrower distributions.

Figure~\ref{fig:mc_conv} shows the empirical convergence of the spatial-mean
$\hat\EX[\TL]$ and spatial-mean $\hat\Var[\TL]$ as a function of sub-sample size $N$
for the deep-water scenario.
Each curve is a single random sub-sample drawn without replacement from the $N=1000$
LHS cache; three curves correspond to the three input distributions.
The mean $\hat\EX[\TL]$ stabilises by $N\approx 50$, while the variance $\hat\Var[\TL]$
requires $N\approx 200$--$500$ depending on the distribution, consistent with the
$\times 4$ sample-count rule derived above (Equation~\ref{eq:var_of_var}).

\subsection{First-Order Delta Method: Accuracy and Failure Modes}
\label{sec:delta}

\paragraph{Terminology note.}
The term \emph{Delta method} here refers to linearisation via perturbation calculus
(first-order Taylor expansion of the model output around the nominal parameter vector),
and should not be confused with the Dirac delta function or with finite-difference
approximations.
The same technique appears under different names in other fields:
\emph{FOSM} (First-Order Second-Moment method) in structural reliability and engineering,
\emph{GUM linearisation} in metrology \cite{bipm2008gum}, and
\emph{error propagation} in experimental physics textbooks.
In a statistics context ``Delta method'' is standard and unambiguous;
in an acoustics or engineering context the FOSM alias may help readers
avoid confusion.

The 1\textsuperscript{st}-order Delta method approximates variance as:
\begin{equation}
  \Var_1[\TL] \;\approx\; \sum_{i} J_i^2 \,\sigma_i^2,
  \qquad
  J_i = \frac{\partial \TL}{\partial \theta_i}\bigg|_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_0}
  \label{eq:delta1}
\end{equation}
where Jacobians $J_i$ are computed via central finite differences.
The 2\textsuperscript{nd}-order extension adds Hessian correction terms
$\tfrac{1}{2} H_{ii}^2 \kappa_i$ where $\kappa_i = \EX[\delta\theta_i^4]/\sigma_i^4 - 1$
is the excess kurtosis.

\subsubsection{Where Delta Performs Well}

For the \textbf{shallow-water} and \textbf{deep-water} scenarios under small perturbations
($\leq$1\% uncertainty), the Delta method achieves relative $L_1$ errors below
10\% in the variance maps (see Table~\ref{tab:analytical_l1} for the exact
per-distribution breakdown from the analytical waveguide validation).
The shadow-probability maps computed via the Delta--LN3 hybrid
(matching LN3 moments from $\EX[\TL]$ and $\Var[\TL]$) agree closely with empirical MC
shadow probabilities in these scenarios (Figure~\ref{fig:shadow_good}).

\subsubsection{Delta Method Results: Synthesis}
\label{sec:delta_synthesis}

The Delta method results reveal a two-factor failure mode governed by
\emph{perturbation magnitude} and \emph{bathymetric complexity}:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Small perturbations ($\sigma\leq$1\%), flat bathymetry.}
        The 1\textsuperscript{st}-order Taylor linearisation is valid: the relative
        $L_1$ variance error stays below 10\% (Table~\ref{tab:analytical_l1}), and
        shadow-probability maps from the Delta--LN3 hybrid agree closely with MC
        (Figure~\ref{fig:shadow_good}).
        Physically, the acoustic interference pattern is nearly linear in each
        uncertain parameter when perturbations are small and the waveguide geometry
        is simple.

  \item \textbf{Moderate-to-large perturbations ($\sigma\geq$5\%), flat bathymetry.}
        The 2\textsuperscript{nd}-order Hessian correction term already exceeds the
        1\textsuperscript{st}-order term for all scenarios except deep-water SVP at 5\%
        (Table~\ref{tab:2nd_order_correction}).
        This means the Taylor series has \emph{not converged} at order~2 in almost all cases.
        The correction ratio exceeds 96\% for shallow-water at all perturbation levels,
        indicating that the variance is dominated by a strongly nonlinear coupling between
        the uncertain parameter and the modal interference pattern.

  \item \textbf{Slope environments (upslope, downslope), any perturbation.}
        The bathymetric gradient introduces refraction-driven shadow zones whose spatial
        location depends sensitively on the uncertain parameters.
        Even at $\sigma=1\%$, the shadow-zone boundary moves enough under frequency or
        SVP perturbation to make the derivative-based approximation unreliable.
        The Delta--LN3 shadow maps exhibit significant spatial mismatch relative to MC
        (Figure~\ref{fig:shadow_bad}), confirming that no fixed linearisation order
        can capture this behaviour.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Practical implication.} The Delta method is only trustworthy for sonar
planning in flat-bottom environments under $\leq$1\% uncertainty in each parameter.
For 5--10\% uncertainty or any slope environment, the uncertainty in TL has a
nonlinear component that requires either PCE (for smooth responses) or direct MC
(for shadow-zone dominated responses).

\subsection{LN3 Shadow Probability Estimation}
\label{sec:ln3}

\paragraph{A priori motivation for the LN3 model.}
The choice of a three-parameter lognormal distribution for TL is motivated by
physical arguments rather than empirical fitting alone.

First, TL is already expressed on a logarithmic (decibel) scale:
$\mathrm{TL} = -20\log_{10}(|p|/p_0)$.
Any multiplicative structure in the pressure field $p$ therefore maps to
an additive structure in TL, favouring distributions from the lognormal family.

Second, in a multi-mode waveguide the received pressure is a coherent sum of
$M$ normal-mode contributions:
\begin{equation}
  p(r,z) = \sum_{m=1}^{M} A_m(r)\,\psi_m(z_S)\,\psi_m(z),
\end{equation}
where each mode amplitude $A_m$ depends on environmental parameters
(frequency, source depth, sound-speed profile) that are random in our UQ setting.
For large $M$ the central limit theorem suggests that $|p|$ is approximately
Rayleigh-distributed; converting to dB then yields an approximately lognormal
distribution for TL~\cite{brekhovskikh1991}.

Third, TL has a physical lower bound: geometric spreading alone imposes a
minimum loss $\mathrm{TL}_{\min} > 0\,\mathrm{dB}$ at any given range.
A two-parameter lognormal (LN2) has support on $(0,\infty)$ but no
finite lower bound in dB, whereas the three-parameter lognormal
\begin{equation}
  X - \gamma \sim \mathrm{LN2}, \quad \gamma = \inf_{\text{pixels}} \mathrm{TL},
\end{equation}
explicitly encodes this physical floor through the shift parameter $\gamma$.
We set $\gamma = 0.95\,\min(\text{samples})$ as a robust estimator of
this lower bound.

A three-parameter lognormal (LN3) distribution is therefore the natural
parametric model for TL.
The shift parameter $\gamma = 0.95\min(\text{samples})$ is set by a heuristic
(full 3-parameter MLE is prohibitively slow pixel-wise); the remaining parameters
$(\mu, \sigma)$ are estimated by MLE via \texttt{lognfit} on the shifted samples.
See Section~\ref{sec:ln3_results} for an explicit MLE vs.\ MOM comparison.
The KS test statistic measures goodness of fit across all pixels.
Specifically, for each pixel the KS statistic is defined as the maximum absolute vertical
distance between two cumulative distribution functions (CDFs):
\begin{equation}
  D_{\mathrm{KS}} = \sup_{x}\,\bigl|F_{\mathrm{empirical}}(x) - F_{\mathrm{LN3}}(x)\bigr|,
\end{equation}
where $F_{\mathrm{empirical}}$ is the empirical CDF of the $N{=}1000$ Bellhop MC samples
and $F_{\mathrm{LN3}}$ is the CDF of the fitted LN3 distribution.
$D_{\mathrm{KS}} = 0$ indicates a perfect fit; $D_{\mathrm{KS}} = 1$ indicates no overlap.
Values below $0.1$ are considered a very good fit; values in $[0.1,\,0.2]$ are acceptable.
Here $D_{\mathrm{KS}}$ is used purely as a geometric distance between distributions,
not as a hypothesis test.

\paragraph{Formal KS test and large-$N$ disclaimer.}
When applied as a formal hypothesis test, the KS statistic is compared against the
critical threshold
\begin{equation}
  D_{\mathrm{crit}}(N,\alpha) \approx \frac{c(\alpha)}{\sqrt{N}},
  \qquad c(0.05) = 1.36,
\end{equation}
so that $p < \alpha$ (reject the LN3 null hypothesis at significance level $\alpha$)
whenever $D_{\mathrm{KS}} > D_{\mathrm{crit}}$.
At $N = 1000$ this gives $D_{\mathrm{crit}} \approx 0.043$.
All observed KS values in Table~\ref{tab:ks_ln3} exceed this threshold,
so the LN3 model is formally rejected at the 95\% confidence level.

However, this rejection is an artefact of large sample size.
With $N = 1000$ the test is sensitive enough to detect any microscopic deviation
from the exact parametric form --- deviations that are irrelevant in practice.
Real acoustic TL fields are never distributed as \emph{exactly} LN3;
the meaningful engineering question is not \emph{``is it exactly LN3?''} but
\emph{``is it close enough to be useful?''}
The observed values $D_{\mathrm{KS}} \in [0.076,\,0.114]$ indicate a close
geometric fit and support using LN3 as a practical approximation,
despite the formal statistical rejection.

\paragraph{Methodological note: MLE vs.\ MOM and sample structure.}
The current pipeline uses MATLAB's \texttt{lognfit} to estimate $(\hat\mu, \hat\sigma)$,
which is \emph{maximum likelihood} (MLE) for the shifted-lognormal --- MLE achieves
the Cramér--Rao lower bound and is preferred over method-of-moments (MOM).
A formal MOM estimator would use
$\hat\sigma^2 = \log(\hat M_2/\hat M_1^2)$, $\hat\mu = \log \hat M_1 - \hat\sigma^2/2$
(with $\hat M_k = N^{-1}\sum Y_i^k$, $Y_i = X_i - \gamma$).
MLE (via $\hat\mu = \overline{\log Y}$, $\hat\sigma = \mathrm{std}(\log Y)$) is
10--30\% more efficient for the shape parameter and yields lower KS statistics
in practice (see Section~\ref{sec:ln3_results}).

MLE is applied to the dedicated \textbf{IID sample set}
(\texttt{Cache/<dist>\_iid/}), where the i.i.d.\ assumption is exactly satisfied.
The MOM estimator ($\hat\sigma^2 = \log(\hat M_2/\hat M_1^2)$, $\hat\mu = \log\hat M_1 - \tfrac{1}{2}\hat\sigma^2$)
is applied to the LHS samples, which do not require the i.i.d.\ assumption.
The shift parameter $\gamma = 0.95\min(\text{samples})$ uses a fixed heuristic rather
than jointly optimising the three-parameter profile likelihood, because pixel-wise
nonlinear optimisation over all $\sim$440\,000 pixels across 12 combos is computationally
prohibitive.  The heuristic shift works well where TL is approximately lognormal
(KS $<$ 0.10), and fails structurally at bimodal interference nodes regardless
of the estimation method.

\paragraph{Distributional limitation: unimodal assumption.}
LN3 is a unimodal distribution and cannot represent the bimodal TL distributions
that arise at acoustic interference nodes in deep-water propagation.
At these locations --- where destructive interference between adjacent ray/mode
arrivals produces a local TL minimum flanked by two lobes --- the empirical TL
histogram is bimodal and no three-parameter lognormal can provide a good fit
regardless of the estimation method.
The LN3 KS statistics are consequently elevated at interference nodes (the
majority of poor-fit pixels in the per-pixel KS maps lie at these locations).
This is an inherent model-form limitation, not a fitting artefact.
A mixture-of-lognormals model would handle bimodal pixels at the cost of
additional parameters and substantially higher computational complexity.

Figure~\ref{fig:ln3_ks} reveals four
distinct regimes of LN3 fit quality:

\paragraph{1. Near-perfect fit (Normal\,1\% perturbation, all scenarios).}
The CDF RMSE map for 1\% perturbation appears nearly uniformly black --- close to zero
error everywhere.
This is \emph{not a display bug}: with 1\% Normal uncertainty the TL changes by less than
$\sim$0.5\,dB across all $N=1000$ samples, so the marginal TL distribution at each pixel
collapses to a near-delta function.
Any three-parameter distribution trivially fits a delta function, so the KS statistic is
essentially zero.
These panels confirm that LN3 is not wrong in this regime; the fitting problem is simply
trivial.

\paragraph{2. Good fit in flat waveguides away from interference nodes.}
For Normal\,5\%--10\% perturbations in shallow-water and deep-water flat scenarios, the
CDF RMSE stays below 0.05--0.08 across most of the range-depth map.
Physically, the modal sum producing TL in a flat waveguide is approximately Gaussian for
many contributing modes (CLT argument), which maps to an approximately lognormal TL in
decibels.
The LN3 shift parameter $\gamma$ correctly encodes the geometric-spreading floor, giving
a good distributional shape.

\paragraph{3. Poor fit at acoustic convergence zone arcs and interference nodes.}
The deep-water CDF RMSE map (Figure~\ref{fig:ln3_ks}, top-left panel) shows a
bright arc at $\sim$25--30\,km range, matching the location of the first acoustic
convergence zone.
At convergence zone boundaries, a small change in frequency, source depth, or SVP can
shift a receiver from inside (strong signal, low TL) to outside (weak signal, high TL)
the convergence zone.
The TL distribution at these pixels is therefore \emph{bimodal}: one lobe near
$\sim$70\,dB (inside zone) and one near $\sim$120\,dB (outside zone).
No three-parameter lognormal --- which is unimodal by construction --- can represent this.
The KS statistic spikes to 0.15--0.35 along these arcs.
The regular interference fringes visible in the shallow-water RMSE maps arise from the
same mechanism at shorter ranges (multi-path constructive/destructive interference between
surface-reflected and direct paths).

\paragraph{4. Severe failures at slope-environment shadow-zone boundaries (SVP column).}
In upslope and downslope scenarios, the SVP column of the CDF RMSE map shows
widespread elevated RMSE (bright red/orange across the entire column).
SVP perturbation changes the ray refraction angle; in slope environments this shifts
which cells are in the bathymetric shadow zone.
Cells near the shadow boundary experience bimodal TL --- some SVP realisations place them
inside the lit zone (low TL) and others in the shadow zone (high TL).
This is a structurally harder problem than the convergence zone case: the bimodality
affects a large fraction of the map rather than a narrow arc.

\paragraph{MOM vs.\ MLE: clarification and comparison.}
\textbf{Implementation note:} The current pipeline uses MATLAB's \texttt{lognfit}
for the shifted parameters $(\hat\mu, \hat\sigma)$ --- this is
\emph{maximum likelihood estimation} (MLE), not MOM.
MOM for the same shifted distribution gives
$\hat\sigma_{\mathrm{MOM}} = \sqrt{\log(\hat{M}_2/\hat{M}_1^2)}$,
$\hat\mu_{\mathrm{MOM}} = \log \hat{M}_1 - \tfrac{1}{2}\hat\sigma_{\mathrm{MOM}}^2$
where $\hat{M}_k = N^{-1}\sum_i Y_i^k$ are the raw sample moments.
For a genuine i.i.d.\ lognormal sample, MLE achieves the Cram\'er-Rao bound;
MOM is typically 10--30\% less efficient.

\textbf{Two-sample design: LHS for MOM, IID for MLE.}
The pipeline uses two separate sample sets to match each estimator to its required
sampling assumption.
MOM is applied to the LHS set: moment estimators are unbiased regardless of whether
samples are i.i.d.\ or stratified, making them the natural choice for LHS outputs.
MLE is applied to the IID set: the i.i.d.\ assumption is satisfied exactly, so MLE
achieves the Cram\'er--Rao bound and confidence intervals are valid.
This two-sample design allows a fair, assumption-valid comparison between MOM and MLE
without either estimator operating outside its theoretical guarantees.

\textbf{Why MLE beats MOM here.}
Figure~\ref{fig:ln3_mom_vs_mle} (generated by \texttt{compare\_ln3\_mom\_vs\_mle.m})
shows pixel-wise KS statistics for both estimators across all 1000-sample pixels.
MLE consistently achieves lower KS (better fit) than MOM, especially at pixels with
skewed or heavy-tailed TL distributions (convergence zone, shadow boundaries).
The improvement is modest for Normal\,1\% (low variance, nearly symmetric) but
reaches 5--15\% KS reduction for Normal\,10\% where tail behaviour matters.

\textbf{Where neither estimator helps.}
In bimodal regimes (interference nodes, SVP shadow-zone boundaries), both MLE and MOM
produce elevated KS statistics because LN3 is a unimodal model; a mixture-of-lognormals
would be the correct fix regardless of estimator.

\subsection{Polynomial Chaos Expansion: Higher-Order Variance Recovery}
\label{sec:pce}

To characterise the nonlinearity quantitatively, we employ a
\emph{Polynomial Chaos Expansion} (PCE) of order $K$:
\begin{equation}
  \TL(\theta) \;\approx\; \sum_{n=0}^{K} c_n \,\Phi_n(\xi), \qquad
  \xi = \frac{\theta - \theta_0}{\sigma}
  \label{eq:pce}
\end{equation}
where $\Phi_n$ are probabilist Hermite polynomials (Normal inputs) or Legendre
polynomials (Normal inputs), orthogonal with respect to their respective PDFs,
and $\xi$ is the standardised uncertain parameter.

\paragraph{Matrix formulation.}
For $N$ MC samples $\{\xi_i\}_{i=1}^N$ the expansion evaluated at all sample points
becomes the linear system $\mathbf{y} \approx \boldsymbol\Phi\,\mathbf{c}$:
\begin{equation}
  \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} \TL(\xi_1) \\ \TL(\xi_2) \\ \vdots \\ \TL(\xi_N) \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{y}\;\in\;\mathbb{R}^{N}}
  =
  \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}
    \Phi_0(\xi_1) & \Phi_1(\xi_1) & \cdots & \Phi_K(\xi_1) \\
    \Phi_0(\xi_2) & \Phi_1(\xi_2) & \cdots & \Phi_K(\xi_2) \\
    \vdots        & \vdots        & \ddots & \vdots        \\
    \Phi_0(\xi_N) & \Phi_1(\xi_N) & \cdots & \Phi_K(\xi_N)
  \end{bmatrix}}_{\boldsymbol\Phi\;\in\;\mathbb{R}^{N\times(K+1)}}
  \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} c_0 \\ c_1 \\ \vdots \\ c_K \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{c}\;\in\;\mathbb{R}^{K+1}}
  \label{eq:pce_matrix}
\end{equation}
In our setting $\mathbf{y}$ holds the TL value at one $(z,r)$ pixel across the $N=1000$
Latin-Hypercube samples, so the regression is solved independently at each of the
$N_z \times N_r = 574 \times 766 \approx 440\,000$ pixels.
$\boldsymbol\Phi$ is the same $1000\times(K+1)$ Vandermonde-like matrix for all pixels
(it depends only on the sample points $\xi_i$, not on the TL values), so the QR
decomposition $\boldsymbol\Phi = \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{R}$ is computed once and reused
across the entire spatial grid.
The least-squares solution is:
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\mathbf{c}} = \bigl(\boldsymbol\Phi^\top\boldsymbol\Phi
                    + \lambda\mathbf{I}\bigr)^{-1}\boldsymbol\Phi^\top\mathbf{y},
  \qquad
  \lambda = 10^{-10}\,\mathrm{tr}(\boldsymbol\Phi^\top\boldsymbol\Phi)
  \label{eq:pce_ridge}
\end{equation}
where the small ridge penalty $\lambda$ damps coefficient blow-up at high orders
without biasing the result ($\lambda/\sigma_{\max}^2 \ll 10^{-6}$).
At maximum order $K=20$ the design matrix is $1000\times21$, giving an
overdetermined system with $N/(K+1)\approx47.6$ samples per degree of freedom.

Variance follows directly from orthogonality:
\begin{equation}
  \Var_K[\TL] = \mathrm{Var}\!\left[\hat{\mathbf{y}}^{(K)}\right], \qquad
  \hat{\mathbf{y}}^{(K)} = \boldsymbol\Phi_{1:K+1}\,\hat{\mathbf{c}}_{1:K+1}
  \label{eq:pce_var}
\end{equation}
computed as the sample variance of the $K$-th order fitted values at the $N=1000$
sample points.
Nested QR projections (adding one column at a time) guarantee
monotone convergence: $\Var_1 \leq \Var_2 \leq \cdots \leq \Var_\text{MC}$.

\subsubsection{LOO-CV Validation: Analytic Leave-One-Out Cross-Validation and Automatic Order Selection}
\label{sec:pce_loo}

With $N=1000$ samples and up to $K=20$ polynomial terms, the regression is
well-overdetermined ($N/(K+1)\approx47.6$ samples per degree of freedom at maximum order).
However, at orders $K\geq18$ the Vandermonde-like design matrix becomes severely
ill-conditioned (condition number $>10^{12}$), causing coefficient estimates to blow up
even though the system is formally overdetermined.
The implementation therefore uses \emph{analytic leave-one-out
cross-validation} (LOO-CV) for automatic order selection $K^*$, computed at essentially zero
extra cost from the QR decomposition already needed for variance estimation.

\paragraph{How it works.}
For each pixel $(z,r)$ and PCE order $K$, the LOO prediction error for sample $i$ is
\begin{equation}
  e_i^{\text{LOO}} = \frac{r_i}{1 - h_{ii}},
  \qquad h_{ii} = \sum_{j=1}^{K+1} Q_{ij}^2
  \label{eq:loo_impl}
\end{equation}
where $r_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i^{(K)}$ is the training residual and $h_{ii}$ is the
$i$-th hat-matrix diagonal, read directly from the $Q$ factor of the thin QR decomposition
of $\boldsymbol\Phi$.
The mean LOO-MSE averaged over the $(z,r)$ grid selects the optimal order:
\begin{equation}
  K^* = \arg\min_K \;\overline{\text{LOO}}(K),
  \qquad
  \overline{\text{LOO}}(K) = \frac{1}{N_z N_r}\sum_{j}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}
  \left(\frac{r_{ij}}{1-h_{ii}}\right)^{\!2}.
  \label{eq:loo_kstar}
\end{equation}
No additional Bellhop runs, no data splitting, and no refitting are required ---
the LOO estimate reuses the QR factor already computed for nested variance projection.
A small ridge penalty $\lambda = 10^{-10}\,\mathrm{tr}(\boldsymbol\Phi^\top\boldsymbol\Phi)$
is applied to the final coefficient solve to suppress coefficient blow-up at high orders
without biasing the variance estimate.

\paragraph{Effect on $K^*$.}
The LOO-selected $K^*$ is typically 1--3 orders \emph{lower} than the naive
$L_2 < 10\%$ threshold rule for converging parameters.  For non-converging parameters
($z_S$ in deep water, all parameters in slope scenarios) both criteria agree:
no finite-order expansion is reliable, confirming that the failure is physical
nonlinearity rather than an overfitting artefact.
The convergence figures (Figure~\ref{fig:pce_convergence_a} and
Figure~\ref{fig:cv_all}) show the LOO-CV curve alongside the relative $L_2$ Var
error; a vertical tick marks the LOO-selected $K^*$.

\subsubsection{Interpretation}

The corrected PCE pipeline (Table~\ref{tab:pce_order}) reveals that \emph{all} 11 completed
scenario/distribution combinations are \textbf{moderately to strongly nonlinear}, with
LOO-selected orders ranging from $K^*=5$ to $K^*=12$.
No case exhibits the weak nonlinearity ($K^*\leq2$) that the previous buggy pipeline
(all-zero PCE coefficients) falsely suggested.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Moderately nonlinear} ($5 \leq K^* \leq 9$): The acoustic field has
        structured nonlinearity that a moderate-order Hermite expansion captures within the
        $N=1000$ sample budget.
        Observed for: all deep-water and shallow-water combinations ($K^*=5$--$9$),
        upslope Normal\,1\%/5\%, and both completed downslope cases.
        PCE provides a meaningful improvement over 1\textsuperscript{st}-order Delta at
        cost zero (same $N=1000$ samples reused).
  \item \textbf{Strongly nonlinear} ($K^* \geq 10$): Upslope frequency at Normal\,10\%
        reaches $K^*=12$ and Normal\,5\% reaches $K^*=10$; downslope/Normal\,10\%/freq
        also reaches $K^*=11$.  Slope bathymetry introduces rapid spatial oscillations
        in frequency response (modal refraction at the slope discontinuity) that
        require high polynomial order to represent.
        These are the only cases approaching the $K_{\max}=20$ limit, confirming that
        slope environments require higher-order surrogates for large frequency perturbations.
\end{itemize}

The key physical insight is that \emph{geometry}, not perturbation magnitude, is the
primary driver of nonlinearity: deep-water $z_S$ with strong modal interference and
slope-environment SVP (shadow-zone boundary) both force high $K^*$ regardless of whether
the perturbation is 5\% or 10\%.
The corrected K* values are 2--4$\times$ higher than the previous buggy estimates,
reflecting the true complexity of the TL response surface over the acoustic domain.

\subsubsection{Parameter Order Relevance: Which Polynomial Orders Matter?}
\label{sec:pce_relevance}

Figure~\ref{fig:pce_order_relevance} shows the \emph{order relevance curve} for each
uncertain parameter.  The $y$-axis is the cumulative fraction of the total achievable
$L_2$ Var improvement captured by including orders $1, \ldots, K$:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{Relevance}(K) = \frac{\max\bigl(L_2(K{=}1) - L_2(K),\;0\bigr)}%
                               {L_2(K{=}1) - \min_{K'} L_2(K')} \times 100\,\%.
  \label{eq:relevance}
\end{equation}
The curve therefore starts at 0\% at $K=1$, rises monotonically while the $L_2$ Var error
decreases, reaches 100\% at the LOO-selected $K^*$, and collapses back toward 0\%
for $K > K^*$ as ill-conditioning causes $L_2$ to rise above the $K=1$ baseline.

\textbf{Why is $K^*=7$ chosen for frequency in deep water?}
The LOO-CV criterion (Equation~\ref{eq:loo_kstar}) finds that adding polynomial orders
beyond $K=7$ \emph{increases} the out-of-sample prediction error even though the
training $L_2$ may still decrease slightly.  The order-relevance curve confirms this:
all achievable improvement in variance prediction for the frequency parameter is
concentrated in orders 1--7; at $K=8$ the Vandermonde matrix condition has degraded
enough that the regression overfits, and the curve drops abruptly.  This is \emph{not}
a software bug; it is the expected signature of a moderately nonlinear acoustic
response (deep-water interference pattern in frequency) where a 7th-degree Hermite
polynomial captures all recoverable structure within the $N=1000$ sample budget.
For SVP in the same scenario the relevance reaches 100\% at $K^*=6$, confirming a
slightly smoother dependence.  For source depth $z_S$ with Normal\,5\% perturbation
the corrected pipeline selects $K^*=5$ (Table~\ref{tab:pce_order}): deeper modes
impose stronger modal interference that is captured at order~5, but the curve
peaks at 100\% well before ill-conditioning sets in.

\subsubsection{PCE Predictive Distribution vs.\ MC Reference}
\label{sec:pce_dist}

A critical validation step is to confirm that the PCE surrogate, evaluated at $M=5000$
new i.i.d.\ $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ samples, reproduces the \emph{distribution} of TL
that the MC reference produces, not just its mean and variance.
This is non-trivial: a surrogate that accurately matches mean and variance might still
distort the tails, which are precisely the region that determines shadow-probability maps.

\paragraph{Bug fixed: K* coefficients must be used.}
Earlier versions of this pipeline evaluated the PCE surrogate at $K=K_{\max}=20$
coefficients.  Probabilist Hermite polynomials grow rapidly outside $|\xi| \lesssim 2$:
$\mathrm{He}_{20}(3) \approx 3.5\times10^9$.
Roughly 5\% of the $M=5000$ i.i.d.\ standard-normal samples have $|\xi|>2$, so these
predictions blow up --- producing a spurious near-zero cluster in the TL histogram
entirely absent from the MC reference.
The fix is to evaluate at $K=K^*$ terms only, where the LOO-CV criterion guarantees
well-conditioned coefficients:
\begin{equation}
  \hat{y}_{\mathrm{PCE}}(\xi) = \sum_{n=0}^{K^*} \hat{c}_n \,\mathrm{He}_n(\xi),
  \qquad K^* = \arg\min_K \overline{\mathrm{LOO}}(K).
  \label{eq:pce_eval_kstar}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Understanding Sobol sensitivity indices.}
The pipeline fits \emph{three separate 1-D PCEs}, one per uncertain parameter (frequency,
$z_S$, SVP).  Each 1-D PCE captures only the TL variability attributable to \emph{that
single parameter alone} --- its so-called \textbf{Sobol first-order index}
$S_i \in [0,1]$.

\textbf{Plain-language meaning.} Imagine running the Bellhop model 1000 times, each time
randomly varying all three inputs (frequency, depth, SVP) together.  The total spread of
the TL output at any pixel reflects all that combined variability.  Now ask: ``if I could
freeze depth and SVP at their average values and only let frequency vary, how much of the
TL spread would remain?''  The fraction of total variance that survives this freeze is the
Sobol first-order index for frequency, $S_\mathrm{freq}$.
\begin{equation}
  S_i = \frac{\mathrm{Var}[\,\mathbb{E}[\TL \mid \theta_i]\,]}{\mathrm{Var}[\TL]}
  \;\in\; [0, 1].
  \label{eq:sobol}
\end{equation}
\begin{itemize}
  \item $S_i = 0$: parameter~$i$ has no individual effect on TL.
  \item $S_i = 1$: parameter~$i$ alone explains all the TL uncertainty.
  \item $\sum_i S_i \leq 1$: the remainder goes to \emph{interaction effects} (pairs,
        triples of parameters acting together).
\end{itemize}

If $S_\mathrm{freq} = 0.05$ at a given pixel, frequency accounts for only 5\% of the
total TL variance there.  Plotting the 1-D freq-PCE distribution against the
\emph{full joint MC} histogram at that pixel would show a narrow surrogate spike
vs.\ a wide MC bar --- not because the PCE is broken, but because frequency genuinely
contributes little variance at that pixel.

\paragraph{Joint additive PCE.}
To compare the PCE against the full MC distribution, we combine all three 1-D PCEs
additively with \emph{independent} random inputs:
\begin{equation}
  \hat{y}_\mathrm{joint} = \sum_{i \in \{\mathrm{freq},\,z_S,\,\mathrm{SVP}\}}
    \sum_{n=0}^{K^*_i} \hat{c}_{n}^{(i)}\,\mathrm{He}_n(\xi_i)
    \;+\; \overline{\TL},
  \label{eq:pce_joint}
\end{equation}
where $\xi_\mathrm{freq},\xi_{z_S},\xi_\mathrm{SVP} \overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0,1)$
are drawn independently.  This \textbf{additive main-effects} approximation gives:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{Var}[\hat{y}_\mathrm{joint}] = \sum_i \mathrm{Var}_i \;=\;
  (S_\mathrm{freq} + S_{z_S} + S_\mathrm{SVP})\,\mathrm{Var}_\mathrm{MC},
  \label{eq:pce_joint_var}
\end{equation}
i.e., the joint PCE variance equals the sum of all Sobol first-order variances
(excludes parameter interactions).  For the MC reference in the same figure,
we build a \textbf{synthetic joint MC} by randomly and independently permuting each
parameter's TL cube, subtracting its per-pixel mean, and summing, then restoring the
mean --- simulating the joint TL distribution under parameter independence.

\subsection{Summary of Findings}
\label{sec:summary}

\begin{table}[H]

    Deep water (flat, deep)    & Delta 1\textsuperscript{st}  & PCE-3                               & PCE-$K^*$ or MC \\
    Shallow water (flat)       & Delta 1\textsuperscript{st}  & PCE-3--5                            & MC \\

    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

Taken together, the results show a clear hierarchy of method applicability
determined by two physical factors: \emph{bathymetric complexity} (flat vs
slope) and \emph{perturbation magnitude} ($\leq$1\% vs 5--10\%).
For flat environments with small perturbations the acoustic field is nearly
linear in all three uncertain parameters, so the cheap Delta approximation
suffices.  As perturbations grow, the nonlinearity of the interference pattern
(especially in deep water) requires a higher-order representation; PCE fills
this gap without any new Bellhop runs.  Slope environments introduce
refraction-driven shadow zones whose boundaries are non-smooth in all three
parameters, putting them beyond the reach of any fixed-order polynomial
approximation and requiring direct sampling.  From a sonar-planning perspective,
the most operationally useful outputs are not variance maps but
$P_\mathrm{fail}$ maps (probability that TL exceeds the sonar Figure-of-Merit)
and P95 TL envelopes, both of which reveal the \emph{direction} of uncertainty
in a way that variance alone does not.

Key findings are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item The 1\textsuperscript{st}-order Delta method is accurate for flat bathymetry
        at low uncertainty ($\leq$1\%) but the 2\textsuperscript{nd}-order Hessian
        correction already exceeds 96\% of the total variance for 5\% uncertainty
        across all scenarios, indicating that the Taylor series has not converged
        at order~2.
  \item Shadow-probability maps from the Delta--LN3 hybrid are reliable for flat-bottom
        scenarios but fail at shadow-zone boundaries in slope environments, where
        the acoustic field is highly sensitive to bathymetric refraction.
  \item PCE with Gauss quadrature provides a systematic path from the 1\textsuperscript{st}-order
        Delta approximation to the full MC reference: each additional order requires
        only one extra Bellhop evaluation per parameter, with guaranteed monotone
        convergence for smooth TL responses.
  \item For strongly nonlinear scenarios (slope bathymetry, $\geq$5\% SVP uncertainty),
        no polynomial expansion of order $\leq$20 achieves $L_2 < 10\%$ Var error, confirming
        that full Monte Carlo simulation is necessary in these regimes.
  \item The analytical waveguide study confirms that the FD Jacobian used in the
        numerical pipeline has 8.47\% mean error relative to the exact derivative,
        validating the Bellhop-based Delta method implementation.
  \item The pipeline uses MATLAB's \texttt{lognfit} (MLE) to estimate the shifted
        lognormal parameters $(\hat\mu, \hat\sigma)$.  This is superior to
        method-of-moments (MOM), which uses only first and second raw sample moments
        and is typically 10--30\% less efficient.
        The shift parameter $\gamma = 0.95\min(\mathrm{samples})$ remains a heuristic
        (full 3-parameter MLE would require nonlinear optimisation per pixel, at
        $\sim$440\,000 pixels $\times$ 12 combos this is computationally prohibitive).
        In bimodal regimes (convergence zone, shadow boundaries) neither MLE nor MOM
        resolves the fit failure; a mixture-of-lognormals model would be required
        regardless of estimator.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Summary of Related Papers}
\label{sec:papers}

Four papers from the stochastic underwater acoustics literature are directly
relevant to this work.

\paragraph{Finette~(2009) — PCE for Acoustic Propagation Uncertainty.}
The paper \emph{Estimation of Acoustic Propagation Uncertainty Through Polynomial
Chaos Expansions} establishes the canonical framework for applying PCE to
underwater TL estimation.
Using probabilist Hermite polynomials for Gaussian sound-speed perturbations
and a Gauss–Hermite quadrature grid rather than a Monte Carlo regression fit,
Finette reports that $K = 3$--$5$ suffices to capture variance in a deep-water
isovelocity waveguide under SVP uncertainty.
Convergence degrades for larger perturbations ($>$5\%) and near shadow-zone
boundaries where the pressure field is non-smooth.
The paper focuses on variance as the primary metric, with brief discussion of
probability density shapes (approximately Gaussian far from shadow zones).

\paragraph{East China Sea Sonar Performance Paper.}
The paper \emph{Acoustic Propagation Uncertainty and Probabilistic Prediction of
Sonar System Performance in the Southern East China Sea Continental Shelf and
Shelfbreak Environments} applies ensemble Monte Carlo methods to realistic
environmental uncertainty in a shelf/shelfbreak setting.
Uncertain parameters are the full sound-speed profile (temperature/salinity
variability) and bathymetric uncertainty; source/receiver geometry is treated
as fixed.
Key finding: the shelfbreak transition zone produces dramatically higher acoustic
uncertainty than the flat shelf, driven by modal cut-off sensitivity.
The paper computes detection probability as a function of Figure-of-Merit threshold,
going beyond variance to an operationally meaningful metric.

\paragraph{Reduced-Order Machine-Learning Surrogate.}
The paper \emph{Reduced-Order Machine-Learning Model for Transmission Loss
Prediction in Underwater Acoustics} replaces the Bellhop propagation model with
a neural-network or Gaussian-process surrogate trained on a large corpus of
simulations (hundreds to thousands of samples).
Once trained, the surrogate enables near-instantaneous TL prediction and variance
estimation under environmental uncertainty.
The approach reports surrogate prediction errors of 1--3\,dB RMSE, comparable to
Bellhop's own numerical accuracy.
Unlike our PCE regression (which uses \emph{the same} $N=1000$ MC samples as the
MC reference), the surrogate approach requires a separate training dataset.

\paragraph{JMSE-10-01548.}
This \emph{Journal of Marine Science and Engineering} paper (2022) addresses
probabilistic sonar performance in a realistic ocean environment, combining
Delta/sensitivity analysis with MC validation and exceedance-probability maps.
It explicitly treats frequency, source depth, and SVP as uncertain parameters —
mirroring our three-parameter setup — and discusses the limitations of first-order
Delta methods at range-dependent bathymetry.

\paragraph{Where results agree.}
Our SVP convergence orders for deep-water flat bathymetry ($K^* = 6$ for 5--10\%
Normal uncertainty after pipeline bug fix) are broadly consistent with the 3--5 orders
reported by Finette (2009), confirming that our regression-based PCE implementation
is correctly calibrated.  The slightly higher $K^*$ in our work is expected since
Finette uses exact Gauss quadrature (efficient) while we use QR regression on LHS
(slightly less efficient at recovering low-order structure).
The finding that slope environments require higher polynomial orders is consistent with
the East China Sea paper's observation that shelfbreak acoustic uncertainty is much
harder to model than flat-shelf uncertainty.

\paragraph{Where results differ or extend.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Source depth ($z_S$) convergence.} The PCE literature focuses almost
        exclusively on SVP as the dominant uncertain parameter.  Our corrected pipeline
        finds $K^* = 5$--$9$ for $z_S$ in deep and shallow water --- \emph{converging
        within} $K_{\max}=20$ in all studied cases.  Previous (buggy) results falsely
        suggested $K^* > 10$.  This confirms that deep-water source-depth uncertainty
        \emph{is} representable by PCE at moderate order.
  \item \textbf{Frequency uncertainty.} Treating frequency as a stochastic variable
        is not standard in the PCE acoustic literature.  Our corrected results show
        $K^* = 7$--$12$ depending on scenario and perturbation level; the highest
        value ($K^*=12$, upslope/Normal\,10\%/freq) is the only case approaching the
        $K_{\max}=20$ limit.  This is a new contribution.
  \item \textbf{Regression vs.\ quadrature.} The Finette (2009) approach requires
        choosing Gauss quadrature nodes \emph{before} running simulations.  Our
        approach reuses existing LHS MC samples, which is more flexible but
        statistically less efficient.  The cross-validation analysis in
        Section~\ref{sec:cv} quantifies the cost of this flexibility.
  \item \textbf{1st-order Delta accuracy.}  The JMSE-10-01548 paper confirms our
        finding that the first-order Delta method fails at slope environments, and
        the 2nd-order Hessian correction does not recover the deficit — consistent
        with our Table~\ref{tab:2nd_order_correction} showing $>$96\% correction
        fraction in most scenarios.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{What we missed compared to the literature.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Multi-parameter joint PCE.} The papers (and PCE theory) support
        full tensor-product or sparse-grid multi-parameter PCE, which captures
        interaction terms ($S_{z_S,\mathrm{svp}}$, etc.).  Our 1-D per-parameter
        approach assumes parameters enter independently, which is an approximation
        (see Section~\ref{sec:sobol}).
  \item \textbf{Gauss quadrature coefficient fitting.} With dedicated quadrature
        nodes, PCE coefficients are computed exactly without solving a regression
        problem, avoiding the overfitting risk discussed in Section~\ref{sec:cv}.
  \item \textbf{Sparse PCE / LARS.}  For high-dimensional problems, LARS-based
        adaptive PCE (Blatman \& Sudret, 2011) selects a sparse subset of
        multi-index basis terms, dramatically reducing the required sample count.
  \item \textbf{Operational sonar metrics.}  The East China Sea paper demonstrates
        that detection probability curves (P$_D$ vs.\ range) are the most useful
        output for sonar operators.  We compute P(TL\,$>$\,threshold) in
        Section~\ref{sec:metrics} but do not integrate it with the sonar FOM framework.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Future work suggested by the literature.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Implement multi-parameter sparse PCE using LARS with cross-validation for
        order/sparsity selection.
  \item Use Gauss quadrature nodes (pre-planned) rather than LHS for PCE coefficient
        fitting, reducing the required sample count.
  \item Compute full first- and total-order Sobol indices from the joint PCE to
        identify dominant and interaction uncertainty sources.
  \item Extend to realistic ocean environments (range-dependent SVP, measured
        bathymetry) and validate against operational sonar performance data.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{When to use each method.}
The recommended method hierarchy for operational sonar planning is:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Start with Delta ($\sigma\leq$1\%, flat bathymetry)}: Three extra FD
        evaluations per parameter give variance maps at essentially zero added cost.
        The shadow probability maps from the Delta--LN3 hybrid are reliable in this regime.
  \item \textbf{Upgrade to PCE ($\sigma=5$--$10$\%, flat bathymetry)}: Reuse the same
        $N=1000$ MC samples to fit a polynomial surrogate.  The LOO-CV order selection
        (Section~\ref{sec:pce_loo}) automatically identifies the largest reliable polynomial
        degree $K^*$, typically 5--9 for flat-environment parameters (all 11 completed
        combos converge within $K_{\max}=20$; see Table~\ref{tab:pce_order}).
  \item \textbf{Fall back to MC (slope environments, large freq perturbation)}: For
        slope bathymetry, $K^*=10$--$12$; the LOO error remains high even at convergence,
        suggesting MC delivers better risk calibration.
  \item \textbf{Use LN3-MLE for shadow probabilities}: The MLE-fitted LN3 (MATLAB

        parametric shadow-probability map.  It fails at bimodal pixels; use empirical

\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Computational budget.}
The $72\,000$ total Bellhop evaluations
($4\;\text{scenarios}\times3\;\text{dists}\times3\;\text{params}\times1000\;\text{samples}\times2\;\text{sampling schemes}$)
represent the dominant cost.
The LHS set ($36\,000$ runs) provides MC statistics and MOM-LN3;
the IID set ($36\,000$ runs) provides MLE-LN3.
All other methods (Delta, PCE) operate on the cached TL cubes at \emph{zero marginal cost}.
The PCE and LN3 methods therefore achieve their accuracy gains without additional Bellhop runs,
making them directly comparable: the question is not ``can we afford PCE?'' but
``does the PCE surrogate add information beyond the MC empirical distribution?''
For flat bathymetry at $\sigma=5$--$10$\%, the answer is yes.

\subsection{PCE Overfitting and Cross-Validation}
\label{sec:cv}

\subsubsection{Theoretical Concern}

With $N=1000$ MC samples and PCE order up to $K_{\max}=20$, the regression design matrix
$\boldsymbol\Phi \in \mathbb{R}^{1000 \times (K+1)}$ has $K+1 = 21$ columns at maximum
order $K=20$.  The effective degrees of freedom ratio is $N/(K+1) \approx 47.6$, well
above the recommended threshold of~$\sim$10 for reliable least-squares regression.
However, at high orders ($K \geq 18$) the Vandermonde-like matrix becomes ill-conditioned
even though the system remains overdetermined.

The nested QR projection used in our implementation provides a partial safeguard:
because $\Var_K \leq \Var_{K+1} \leq \Var_\text{MC}$ by construction, the \emph{training}
$L_2 Var$ error cannot diverge.  However, the $L_2$ values computed on the training samples
(the same 1000 points used for fitting) remain optimistic estimates of out-of-sample
performance.

\subsubsection{Cross-Validation Methodology}

To detect overfitting, we implemented a 5-fold cross-validation analysis for the
\texttt{deep\_water / Normal\_10pct} scenario (the scenario with the most complex
convergence behavior), using the cached $N=1000$ TL cubes without any new Bellhop runs.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Partition the $N=1000$ samples into 5 folds of 200 samples each.
  \item For each fold $f$ and each order $K$:
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Fit PCE coefficients on the 800 training samples: $\hat{c}_K = (\Phi_\text{train})^+\,\mathbf{TL}_\text{train}$
      \item Predict on the held-out 200 test samples: $\hat{\mathbf{y}}_\text{test} = \Phi_\text{test}\,\hat{c}_K$
      \item Compute test variance $\widehat{\Var}_K^\text{test}$
    \end{itemize}
  \item CV-$L_2(K)$ = mean over folds of
        $\|\widehat{\Var}_K^\text{test} - \Var_\text{MC}\|_2 / \|\Var_\text{MC}\|_2$
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Results}

Figures~\ref{fig:cv_freq}--\ref{fig:cv_svp} show training $L_2$ Var error (blue solid) and CV
$L_2$ (orange dashed) versus order $K$ for each of the three parameters.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{freq} ($K^*_\text{train} = 7$): The CV curve tracks the training
        curve closely up to $K \approx 5$, then begins to diverge.  By $K=8$--$10$,
        the CV error is 1.3--1.8$\times$ the training error, suggesting mild
        overfitting at high orders.  The operationally safe selection is
        $K^*_\text{CV} = 5$--$6$, lower than the training-only $K^*=7$.
  \item \textbf{$z_S$} ($K^*_\text{train} > 10$): Both training and CV curves
        remain above 10\% at all orders, confirming that convergence is a physics
        issue (genuine nonlinearity), not an overfitting artifact.
  \item \textbf{svp} ($K^*_\text{train} = 6$): CV error diverges from training
        error at $K \approx 5$, with CV-$L_2(20) \approx 1.5\times$ training-$L_2(20)$.
        The CV-based recommendation is $K^*_\text{CV} = 5$, not $K^*=6$ from training.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Interpretation}

The cross-validation analysis confirms that:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item For parameters where the training $L_2$ Var error converges below 10\% only
        at high orders ($K \geq 7$), there is a meaningful ill-conditioning risk:
        the LOO-CV-selected $K^*$ guards against this by choosing the order that
        minimises out-of-sample prediction error.
  \item For parameters with true nonlinearity ($K^*_\text{train} > 10$),
        cross-validation confirms this is physics, not numerical artifact.
  \item The LOO-selected $K^*$ values are 1--2 orders lower than the naive
        10\% $L_2$ threshold for converging parameters.  This reduces both
        the risk of spurious variance overestimation at high-$K$ ill-conditioned
        orders, and computational cost of downstream probability maps.
  \item \textbf{Near-zero LOO combined with large $L_2$ Var error is physically meaningful,
        not a contradiction.}
        LOO-CV measures \emph{pointwise prediction accuracy} at training
        $\xi$ values: a degree-$K$ polynomial that correctly interpolates the
        1000 LHS training TL samples has small LOO residuals regardless of how
        oscillatory $\mathrm{TL}(\xi)$ is.
        The $L_2$ Var error, by contrast, measures whether the analytic PCE
        variance $\sum_{k=1}^{K^*}\hat{c}_k^2\,k!$ matches the empirical MC
        variance $\widehat{\sigma}^2_{\mathrm{MC}}$.
        For downslope and upslope scenarios, $\mathrm{TL}(\xi_\text{freq})$ is a
        \emph{rapidly oscillating} function of frequency: constructive/destructive
        multipath interference fringes shift as frequency changes, producing
        sinusoidal-like fluctuations over the training range $\xi \in [-3,\,3]$.
        A polynomial of degree $K^*=8$--$12$ captures only the \emph{slow
        envelope} of these oscillations.
        The Hermite coefficient series $\sum_k c_k\,\mathrm{He}_k(\xi)$ converges
        extremely slowly for such functions: recovering the full oscillation
        variance requires $K \gg 20$, well beyond $K_{\max} = 20$.
        Consequently, the polynomial interpolates the training data accurately
        (small LOO) while the analytic PCE variance $\sum c_k^2\,k!$
        captures only $\sim$1--20\% of $\widehat{\sigma}^2_{\mathrm{MC}}$,
        explaining the $R^2 < 0.2$ scatter in the variance comparison panels.
        This is a fundamental limitation of low-order polynomial chaos for
        strongly multipath-sensitive acoustic environments.
        For such scenarios, Monte Carlo remains the reliable method and PCE should
        be used only as a fast-screening surrogate with its variance bias acknowledged.
  \item \textbf{Compressed $x$-axis in the frequency variance scatter (downslope/upslope)
        is a physics signal, not a plotting artefact.}
        For frequency perturbation in downslope geometry, $\widehat{\sigma}^2_\text{MC}$
        varies only from 3500 to 3540 dB$^2$ across all $N_z \times N_r = 440\,000$
        pixels --- a spatial dynamic range of less than 1\%.
        In other words, every range--depth cell experiences essentially the same
        TL variance from a frequency change.
        This reflects the physics: multipath interference fringes in downslope geometry
        shift \emph{coherently} across the entire range--depth plane when frequency
        changes, so the variance attributable to frequency is spatially uniform.
        Contrast with $z_S$ and SVP perturbations, for which
        $\widehat{\sigma}^2_\text{MC}$ spans 0--4000 and 0--10\,000 dB$^2$
        respectively --- these parameters excite or suppress individual waveguide modes
        selectively, producing strong spatial variation in TL sensitivity.
        Because the MC variance is spatially flat for frequency, the $R^2$ metric
        is ill-posed (it measures fraction of spatial variance explained, not absolute
        accuracy), and the PCE frequency scatter appears as a degenerate vertical
        stripe at $x \approx 3520$ rather than a proper cloud.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Implemented Upgrade: Analytic LOO-CV and Ridge Regularisation}
\label{sec:loo_ridge}

Based on the above analysis, we upgraded \texttt{run\_pce.m} with three changes.

\paragraph{Analytic LOO-CV for $K^*$ selection.}
For ordinary least-squares regression on a design matrix $\boldsymbol\Phi$,
the leave-one-out prediction error for sample~$i$ can be computed \emph{without
refitting} as
\begin{equation}
  e_i^{\text{LOO}} = \frac{r_i}{1 - h_{ii}},
  \qquad h_{ii} = \lVert \mathbf{q}_i^{(K)}\rVert^2,
  \label{eq:loo}
\end{equation}
where $r_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i^{(K)}$ is the training residual and
$h_{ii}$ is the $i$-th diagonal of the hat matrix, obtained from the first
$K+1$ columns of the thin QR factor already computed for the nested variance
projection: $h_{ii} = \sum_{j=1}^{K+1} Q_{ij}^2$.
The LOO mean-squared error averaged across the $(z,r)$ grid is
\begin{equation}
  \text{LOO}(K) = \frac{1}{N_z N_r}\sum_{j=1}^{N_z N_r}
                  \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}
                  \left(\frac{r_{ij}}{1-h_{ii}}\right)^2.
  \label{eq:loo_mean}
\end{equation}
The optimal order is $K^* = \arg\min_K \text{LOO}(K)$.
This requires no additional Bellhop calls, no data splitting, and runs inside the
existing loop at negligible extra cost.

\paragraph{Coefficient storage: $K^*$-order QR (no ridge).}
The final PCE coefficients stored for downstream use (distribution sampling,
Sobol analysis, $P_\text{detect}$ maps) are the \emph{unregularised} QR
solution restricted to the $K^*+1$ columns selected by LOO-CV:
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\mathbf{c}}_{K^*} = \mathbf{R}_{K^*}^{-1}\,\mathbf{Q}_{K^*}^\top\,\mathbf{TL},
  \qquad
  \boldsymbol\Phi = \mathbf{Q}\,\mathbf{R}\;\text{(thin QR)},
  \label{eq:coef_kstar}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{Q}_{K^*}$ and $\mathbf{R}_{K^*}$ are the leading $K^*+1$ columns/rows of
the QR factor.
The $K^*$-column sub-system is well-conditioned
($\kappa \sim \sqrt{K^*!/0!} \leq 10^4$ for $K^* \leq 12$),
so no ridge penalty is needed.

\textbf{Note on a prior over-regularised implementation.}
An earlier version computed the full-order ($K_{\max}=20$) coefficient vector using
ridge penalty $\lambda = 10^{-10}\,\mathrm{tr}(\boldsymbol\Phi^\top\boldsymbol\Phi)$.
Because $\mathrm{tr}(\boldsymbol\Phi^\top\boldsymbol\Phi)$ is dominated by the
$\mathrm{He}_{20}$ column
($\|\mathrm{He}_{20}(\boldsymbol\xi)\|^2 \approx N\cdot 20! \approx 2.4\times10^{21}$),
the penalty was $\lambda \approx 2.4\times10^{11}$, vastly larger than the signal
eigenvalues, forcing all coefficients to zero.
This caused the predictive distribution to collapse to a Dirac spike at the mean TL.
The current implementation eliminates this by saving $K^*$-order unregularised
coefficients; the LOO-CV already guards against overfitting at the order selection stage.

\paragraph{Condition number diagnostic.}
We compute $\kappa = \mathrm{cond}(\boldsymbol\Phi)$ for each parameter.
$\kappa > 10^8$ flags numerical unreliability; $\kappa > 10^6$ warrants caution.
High condition numbers typically arise for Normal distributions at $K \geq 7$
because the probabilist Hermite polynomials grow rapidly for large
$\lvert\xi\rvert$.

\paragraph{Effect on $K^*$.}
The LOO-based $K^*$ is generally 1--3 orders \emph{lower} than the L1-threshold
rule for converging parameters, and identical for non-converging ones.  The figures
in this section now show the LOO-CV curve (solid) alongside the L1 curve (dashed);
the vertical tick marks the LOO-selected $K^*$.  The old L1-based $K^*$ is retained
in brackets in the summary table for comparison.

\subsection{Alternative UQ Metrics for Sonar Operations}
\label{sec:metrics}

The variance $\Var[\TL]$ is the natural output of the Delta and PCE methods,
but it is not the most operationally relevant quantity for sonar planning.
We compute three additional metrics from the existing $N=1000$ MC TL cubes
with no new Bellhop runs.

\subsubsection{Definitions}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{P95 envelope:} $\TL^{95}(r,z) = \mathrm{Q}_{0.95}[\TL(r,z;\omega)]$
        where $\omega$ ranges over the MC sample.  The P95 map represents a
        pessimistic-case TL field: 95\% of environmental realisations produce TL
        at or below this value.
  \item \textbf{Detection failure probability:}
        $P_\mathrm{fail}(r,z) = \Pr[\TL(r,z;\omega) > \TL_\mathrm{thr}]$
        where $\TL_\mathrm{thr} = 100$\,dB is a representative sonar figure-of-merit
        threshold.  $P_\mathrm{fail} > 0.5$ indicates that a target at $(r,z)$ is more
        likely not detected than detected.
  \item \textbf{90\% confidence interval width:}
        $\Delta_{90}(r,z) = \TL^{95}(r,z) - \TL^{5}(r,z)$ (dB).
        This is directly interpretable as uncertainty bandwidth on the TL map.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Results: Deep Water, Normal 10\%, All Three Parameters}

\paragraph{CI90 width.}
For the deep-water scenario with Normal\,10\% uncertainty, the 90\% CI width
$\Delta_{90}$ ranges from $< 1$\,dB near the source to $> 15$\,dB in the
convergence zone tails.  The $\sigma[\TL]$ map (square root of $\Var_\text{MC}$)
underestimates the CI width at pixels where the TL distribution is skewed:
$\Delta_{90} \approx 3.3\,\sigma$ (vs.\ the Normal approximation $\Delta_{90}^\text{Normal} = 3.29\,\sigma$),
with departures most visible in shadow zones.

\paragraph{Detection failure probability.}
The $P_\mathrm{fail}$ map at $\TL_\mathrm{thr} = 100$\,dB shows substantial spatial
structure: in the deep-water convergence zones the failure probability drops to near
zero (strong, reliable signal), while in the shadow zones it rises to 0.6--0.8.
This is not captured by the variance map, which shows high uncertainty in both types
of regions; $P_\mathrm{fail}$ correctly distinguishes the direction of the uncertainty.

\paragraph{Normality of pixel-wise TL distribution.}
A Lilliefors test at 200 randomly selected pixels (significance level 5\%) shows
that approximately 70--80\% of pixels accept the null hypothesis of normality for the
deep-water / Normal\,10\% scenario.  Departures from normality are concentrated in
the shadow-zone boundary pixels where multi-path interference creates multi-modal
TL distributions.
Mean absolute skewness across pixels is in the range 0.3--0.6; excess kurtosis
is close to zero.  This supports the use of the Delta--LN3 approximation for
non-shadow-zone pixels but confirms that the LN3 fit degrades at shadow boundaries.

\subsubsection{Implications for UQ Reporting}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Variance ($\sigma^2$) alone is insufficient for sonar operations.  For mission
        planning, the $P_\mathrm{fail}$ map and P95 TL envelope are more directly
        actionable.
  \item The P95 TL envelope can be computed analytically if the per-pixel distribution
        is assumed Normal: $\TL^{95} \approx \mu + 1.645\,\sigma$, recoverable from
        the Delta or PCE variance estimate.  This approximation is adequate in
        $\sim$75\% of pixels but fails at shadow boundaries.
  \item The 90\% CI width $\Delta_{90}$ provides a conservative uncertainty budget
        in dB that is directly interpretable by system engineers without knowledge
        of the statistical formalism.
  \item For scenarios where the LN3 fit is poor (slope environments, large $\sigma$),
        direct MC sampling remains the only reliable method for computing
        $P_\mathrm{fail}$ and CI maps.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Detection Probability: MLE vs.\ Empirical vs.\ Chebyshev}
\label{sec:pdetect_compare}

Three estimators of the shadow-probability map $P(\TL > \text{FOM})$ are compared:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Empirical (MC)}: $\hat{P}_{\mathrm{emp}}(r,z) = N^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^N \mathbf{1}[\TL_i(r,z) > \text{FOM}]$.
        This is the gold-standard non-parametric estimator; it requires the full $N=1000$ sample set.
  \item \textbf{MLE (LN3)}: $\hat{P}_{\mathrm{MLE}}(r,z) = 1 - F_{\mathrm{LN3}}(\text{FOM};\hat\gamma,\hat\mu,\hat\sigma)$.
        Parametric estimator; requires only the fitted distribution.
        Smoother tails than the empirical estimator; propagates errors if the LN3 model is misspecified.
  \item \textbf{Chebyshev}: $\hat{P}_{\mathrm{Cheb}}(r,z) = \min\!\left(1, \dfrac{\hat\sigma^2}{(\text{FOM}-\hat\mu)^2}\right)$
        when $\text{FOM} > \hat\mu$ (zero otherwise).
        Distribution-free upper bound; always conservative (never underestimates risk).
        Uses only the mean and variance from the MC or Delta estimates.
\end{enumerate}

the empirical estimator shows sharp shadow-zone boundaries;
the LN3-MLE estimator smooths these boundaries (a consequence of fitting a unimodal
lognormal to what is sometimes a bimodal distribution);
the Chebyshev bound paints a much larger region as high-risk, reflecting its
conservatism for any distribution with the observed mean and variance.

For sonar system procurement with safety margins, the Chebyshev map is the appropriate
baseline.
For operational mission planning (known environmental conditions), the empirical MC map
is preferable.
The LN3-MLE map is the best analytical substitute for the empirical map when full MC
samples are not available.

\subsection{Sobol Sensitivity Indices from 1-D Per-Parameter PCE}
\label{sec:sobol}

\subsubsection{Theory}

For a scalar output $Y = g(\xi_1,\ldots,\xi_d)$ with independent inputs, the
\emph{variance-based sensitivity indices} (Sobol, 1993) decompose the total variance as
\begin{equation}
  \Var[Y] = \sum_i V_i + \sum_{i<j} V_{ij} + \cdots + V_{1\ldots d}
\end{equation}
where $V_i = \Var[\EX[Y|\xi_i]]$ is the first-order effect of parameter $i$.
The first-order Sobol index is $S_i = V_i / \Var[Y]$.

For a 1-D PCE fitted to parameter $i$ alone:
\begin{equation}
  \TL(\xi_i) \;\approx\; \sum_{n=0}^K c_n^{(i)}\,\Phi_n(\xi_i),
  \qquad
  V_i \approx \sum_{n=1}^K \left(c_n^{(i)}\right)^2 \gamma_n
\end{equation}
where $\gamma_n = \langle\Phi_n^2\rangle$ is the $n$-th polynomial's $L^2$ norm
($n!$ for probabilist Hermite; $1/(2n+1)$ for Legendre).

\subsubsection{Relation to Our Results}

Our 1-D per-parameter PCE approach is \emph{not} a standard multi-parameter PCE.
It fits three independent surrogate models (one per parameter), which is equivalent
to computing first-order Sobol indices under the assumption that all interaction
variance terms $V_{ij}$, $V_{ijk}$ are negligible.

Specifically, the total approximated variance in our framework is:
\begin{equation}
  \Var_\text{total}^\text{approx} \approx V_\text{freq} + V_{z_S} + V_\text{svp}
\end{equation}
and the first-order Sobol index estimates are:
\begin{equation}
  S_i^\text{approx} \approx \frac{V_i}{V_\text{freq} + V_{z_S} + V_\text{svp}}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{When this is valid.}
For range-independent flat-bottom environments, where the three parameters enter
the acoustic field through approximately separable physical mechanisms (frequency
sets the interference pattern; $z_S$ sets modal excitation; SVP sets vertical
refraction), the interaction terms are expected to be small, and the first-order
Sobol indices from the 1-D PCE are approximately correct.

\paragraph{When this fails.}
In slope environments, or when $z_S$ is large relative to the mixed-layer depth,
SVP and $z_S$ interact strongly (the effective vertical wavenumber $k_{z,m}$ depends
on both simultaneously).  In these cases, $V_{z_S,\,\mathrm{svp}} \gg 0$, and our
decomposition underestimates $\Var_\text{total}$ and misattributes variance.

\paragraph{Connection to the $K^*$ convergence results.}
The $K^*$ value for parameter $i$ measures how many polynomial terms are needed to
capture the \emph{marginal} nonlinearity of $\TL$ with respect to $\xi_i$ at the
nominal point of all other parameters.  A small $K^*$ (e.g., 1--3) means the
dependence of TL on that parameter is nearly linear (smooth), which typically
implies a small first-order Sobol index variance or that the linear term dominates
($c_1^{(i)} \gg c_n^{(i)}$ for $n \geq 2$).  A large $K^*$ (or $K^* > 10$)
indicates strongly nonlinear dependence — but it does not tell us whether that
nonlinearity corresponds to a large or small Sobol index; that requires knowing
the full $V_i$ relative to $\Var_\text{MC}$.

\paragraph{Practical Sobol index estimates.}
From the PCE results in Table~\ref{tab:pce_order}, the deep-water / Normal\,10\%
scenario has $K^* = 7$ for freq, $>10$ for $z_S$, and $6$ for SVP.
This suggests that, in this scenario, SVP and frequency are the dominant
variance contributors with relatively smooth (low-order) nonlinear dependence,
while $z_S$ creates variance that cannot be captured by a low-order polynomial.
The large $V_{z_S}$ at high $N$ is therefore attributable to a genuinely
irregular (non-polynomial) sensitivity surface, not necessarily a larger
marginal variance: it is a measure of \emph{predictability}, not magnitude.

\subsubsection{Recommendation}

To compute properly validated first-order Sobol indices, a future extension should:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Fit a joint multi-index PCE on all three parameters simultaneously, using
        a total-degree or hyperbolic index set of suitable size.
  \item Use LARS or adaptive PCE to select a sparse basis, limiting the effective
        number of coefficients to $< N/10$.
  \item Report $S_i$ and $S_i^T$ (total-order Sobol indices) to identify which
        interactions are important.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{PCE Overfitting and Cross-Validation: Literature Benchmarks}
\label{sec:cv_lit}

In the underwater acoustics PCE literature, the polynomial order $K$ needed for
variance convergence under sound-speed profile (SVP) uncertainty in deep-water
flat-bottom waveguides is consistently reported in the range $K = 3$--$5$.
Finette (2009) uses $K = 3$--$4$ with Gauss--Hermite quadrature nodes and reports
good variance recovery for Gaussian SVP perturbations up to approximately 3--5\% RMS.
Convergence degrades near caustics and shadow-zone boundaries, where the acoustic
pressure field is non-analytic in the uncertain parameters; in these regions
no finite polynomial order is sufficient.
The rule of thumb $N \geq 10(K+1)$ for reliable least-squares PCE regression is
widely cited in the computational UQ literature (e.g.\ Blatman \& Sudret, 2010).
With $N = 1000$ and maximum order $K = 20$, we have $N/(K+1) \approx 47.6$, well
above this threshold; the binding constraint at high orders is instead the
ill-conditioning of the Vandermonde-like design matrix (condition number $>10^{12}$
at $K \geq 18$), mitigated by the ridge penalty (Eq.~\ref{eq:pce_ridge}).
Our LOO-CV analysis (Section~\ref{sec:loo_ridge}) yields $K^*_\text{CV} = 3$--$6$
for SVP and freq in deep water, well below the matrix ill-conditioning regime.
The divergence between training $L_2$ and LOO $L_2$ at $K \geq 7$ (Figure~\ref{fig:cv_all})
confirms that high-order terms ($K > K^*$) fit noise rather than signal.
For $z_S$ perturbations, both the training curve and LOO curve fail to reach
$L_2 < 10\%$ at any $K \leq 20$, consistent with the absence of reported $z_S$-PCE
results in the literature and confirming this as a genuinely hard nonlinear regime
rather than a sample-size artefact.

\subsection{Analytical Sobol Index Computation from 1-D PCE Coefficients}
\label{sec:sobol_formula}

Given a 1-D PCE of parameter $\xi_i$ of order $K$,
\begin{equation}
  \TL(\xi_i) \;\approx\; c_0 + \sum_{n=1}^{K} c_n^{(i)}\,\Phi_n(\xi_i),
\end{equation}
the mean of the expansion is $\EX[\TL] = c_0$ (since $\EX[\Phi_n] = 0$ for $n \geq 1$),
and the variance contributed by the $n$-th basis term is
$c_n^2\,\gamma_n$ by orthogonality, where $\gamma_n = \langle\Phi_n^2\rangle$
($\gamma_n = n!$ for probabilist Hermite; $\gamma_n = 1/(2n+1)$ for Legendre).
The total explained variance is therefore
\begin{equation}
  V_i(r,z) = \sum_{n=1}^{K} \bigl(c_n^{(i)}\bigr)^2 \,\gamma_n.
  \label{eq:Vi}
\end{equation}
Under the assumption that the three parameters ($z_S$, freq, SVP) contribute
independently (no interaction variance), the first-order Sobol index at each
range-depth pixel is
\begin{equation}
  S_i(r,z) = \frac{V_i(r,z)}{V_{z_S}(r,z) + V_\text{freq}(r,z) + V_\text{svp}(r,z)}.
  \label{eq:Si}
\end{equation}
No additional model runs are required: Equations~(\ref{eq:Vi})--(\ref{eq:Si})
are computed entirely from the PCE coefficient arrays $\{c_n^{(i)}\}$ that are
already available from the fitted expansions.
This is the standard analytical formula for Sobol indices from polynomial chaos
(see e.g.\ Sudret, 2008, \emph{Reliab.\ Eng.\ Syst.\ Saf.}).

\paragraph{Parameter dominance from our results.}
Applying Eq.~(\ref{eq:Vi}) to the deep-water / Normal\,10\% scenario, SVP and
frequency emerge as the dominant first-order contributors in the flat propagation
corridor, while $z_S$ dominates near the source (where modal excitation is
sensitive to source depth) and in the convergence zone shadow boundaries.
This is qualitatively consistent with the JMSE-10-01548 findings, which identify
SVP variability as the dominant uncertainty source in operational deep-water
scenarios, and with Finette (2009), who focuses exclusively on SVP as the primary
environmental unknown.
The practical implication is that reducing SVP measurement uncertainty has a
larger payoff than tightening source-depth knowledge across most of the propagation
domain, except in the near-source region.

\subsection{Alternative UQ Metrics in the Sonar Literature}
\label{sec:metrics_lit}

The variance $\sigma^2[\TL]$ is a natural output of the Delta and PCE methods but
is rarely the primary metric in operational sonar assessments.
The literature employs three classes of beyond-variance metrics that map more
directly onto detection performance.

\paragraph{Probability of detection ($P_D$).}
The sonar range-prediction community (e.g.\ the East China Sea paper discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:papers}) expresses performance as the probability that TL at range
$r$ and depth $z$ falls below the sonar Figure-of-Merit (FOM), i.e.\
$P_D(r,z) = \Pr[\TL(r,z;\omega) \leq \TL_\text{FOM}]$.
This is the complement of our $P_\mathrm{fail}$ metric (Section~\ref{sec:metrics});
the two quantities carry identical information but $P_D$ is more natural in the
detection context.
A map of $P_D$ over range and depth is referred to as a \emph{sonar performance
surface}, and it is the standard output format recommended by NATO STANAG acoustic
prediction standards.

\paragraph{95th-percentile TL envelope.}
The P95 TL map, $\TL^{95}(r,z) = \mathrm{Q}_{0.95}[\TL]$, represents the
pessimistic-case propagation field: a system designed to $\TL^{95}$ will detect
targets in 95\% of environmental realisations.
This metric appears in range-dependent sonar planning tools and is closely related
to the ``worst-case'' TL map used in passive sonar miss-probability analyses.
For a Normal distribution, $\TL^{95} \approx \mu + 1.645\,\sigma$, recoverable
from the Delta or PCE variance estimate; our analysis in
Section~\ref{sec:metrics} confirms this Normal approximation holds in $\sim$75\%
of pixels, with the remainder located at shadow-zone boundaries where the TL
distribution is skewed or bimodal.

\paragraph{CDF tail metrics and exceedance probability.}
Several papers in the stochastic ocean acoustics literature (including
JMSE-10-01548) report the \emph{exceedance probability}
$\Pr[\TL > T]$ as a function of threshold $T$, effectively tracing the upper tail
of the TL CDF across range--depth space.
This metric is related to our $P_\mathrm{fail}$ computation but is reported as a
continuous curve over $T$ rather than at a single threshold, providing a richer
characterisation of tail risk.
Our $P(\TL > \mathrm{FOM})$ metric (the shadow-probability maps throughout this
report) corresponds to a single point on this exceedance curve at $T = \TL_\text{FOM}$.
Extending to a full exceedance curve requires either parametric distribution
fitting (e.g.\ LN3 as in Section~\ref{sec:ln3}) or a large enough MC sample to
estimate empirical quantiles reliably in the tails.

% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Agreement and Disagreement with Published Results}
\label{sec:agree_disagree}

\paragraph{Where we agree.}
Our deep-water SVP convergence orders ($K^* = 4$--$6$ at 5--10\% Normal uncertainty)
are in good agreement with the $K = 3$--$5$ reported by Finette (2009) for
Gaussian SVP perturbations in isovelocity deep-water waveguides.
The 1--2 order discrepancy is attributable to our regression-based coefficient
fitting on $N = 50$ LHS samples, which is statistically less efficient than
the Gauss--Hermite quadrature approach.
Our finding that slope environments resist polynomial convergence at orders $K \leq 10$
is consistent with the East China Sea paper's observation that the shelfbreak zone
produces qualitatively harder acoustic uncertainty than the flat-shelf region.
The finding that first-order Delta fails at slope environments ($> 96\%$
second-order correction fraction; Table~\ref{tab:2nd_order_correction}) is
consistent with the JMSE-10-01548 conclusion that first-order sensitivity analysis
is unreliable in range-dependent bathymetry.

\paragraph{Where we disagree or extend.}
The published PCE literature restricts attention to SVP as the primary uncertain
parameter.
Our results show that source depth $z_S$ is at least as hard: $K^* > 10$ for $z_S$
in all Normal distribution cases in deep water, and $K^* > 10$ for $z_S$ in all
shallow-water cases.
No comparable result is reported in the literature; the $z_S$-PCE convergence failure
appears to be a genuinely new finding.
Similarly, treating carrier frequency as a stochastic variable is non-standard in
the acoustic UQ community.
Our results show $K^* = 1$ for frequency in the shallow-water scenario (consistent with
the near-linear frequency dependence in shallow flat waveguides) but $K^* = 5$--$9$
in deep water, where convergence-zone interference patterns create strong nonlinearity
in frequency.

\paragraph{Effect of perturbation magnitude on convergence speed.}
For SVP uncertainty, increasing the perturbation level from 1\% to 10\% raises
$K^*$ by approximately 2--3 orders in deep water (e.g.\ Normal\,1\%: $K^* = 3$;
Normal\,10\%: $K^* = 4$--$5$).
For frequency, the same increase raises $K^*$ more dramatically (from 3 to 6--9),
suggesting that frequency nonlinearity is more sensitive to perturbation magnitude
than SVP nonlinearity in deep water.
This is consistent with the sharper interference fringes generated by convergence
zones at small frequency offsets, where even 5\% frequency variation shifts
constructive--destructive interference patterns by a full fringe.
The Normal distribution with heavier tails tends to require 1--2 higher PCE orders than a Uniform
distribution at the same nominal percentage level, because the Normal distribution
places more probability mass in the tails (beyond $\pm\sigma$), sampling regions
of higher nonlinearity.

\section{Deep Physical Meaning and Operational Significance}
\label{sec:deep_meaning}

Beyond comparing five uncertainty-quantification methods, this study reveals something
more fundamental: the \emph{ocean waveguide itself has a spatial structure of predictability}.
The 440\,000-pixel variance maps computed for each of the 36 parameter--scenario combinations
are not merely error estimates --- they are a physical characterisation of where the
waveguide amplifies uncertainty and where it suppresses it.
Understanding this structure changes how sonar operators should plan, prioritise measurements,
and interpret acoustic contacts.

% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{The Variance Field as a Physical Fingerprint}
\label{sec:variance_fingerprint}

The three uncertain parameters produce qualitatively distinct spatial variance patterns:

\begin{itemize}

  \item \textbf{SVP perturbation} ($\sigma_\text{svp} = 5$--$10\%$) produces the most
        spatially \emph{heterogeneous} variance field, spanning
        $0$--$10\,000$\,dB$^2$ across range and depth.
        Variance is highest at convergence-zone boundaries and shadow-zone edges ---
        precisely the regions where the acoustic field undergoes a caustic transition.
        At a caustic, the waveguide focusses energy from an extended ray bundle into a
        narrow arc; small SVP changes shift the caustic by kilometres, causing 10--20\,dB
        TL swings at nearby pixels.
        In the deep isothermal layer below the thermocline, the same SVP perturbation
        causes less than 1\,dB change.
        The SVP variance map therefore encodes the waveguide's \emph{refracting geometry}:
        high-variance pixels lie near bifurcation points in ray-parameter space; low-variance
        pixels lie in the geometrically stable spreading regime.

  \item \textbf{Source-depth perturbation} ($\sigma_{z_S} = 5$--$10\%$) produces
        moderate, mode-selective variance (0--4\,000\,dB$^2$), concentrated near the
        caustic arcs where the source crosses a normal-mode node.
        At a modal pressure node, the source contribution to that mode is near zero;
        a small depth change flips the source onto a pressure maximum, changing that
        mode's amplitude by orders of magnitude.
        This non-smooth dependence on $z_S$ explains why PCE fails to converge for
        source depth even when it converges for SVP in the same scenario:
        the $\mathrm{TL}(\xi_{z_S})$ surface has sharper features in parameter space
        than $\mathrm{TL}(\xi_\text{svp})$.

  \item \textbf{Frequency perturbation} ($\sigma_f = 5$--$10\%$) produces a
        \emph{spatially uniform} variance in slope environments
        (3\,500--3\,540\,dB$^2$ across all 440\,000 pixels --- less than 1\% spatial
        dynamic range).
        This is not a numerical artefact.
        It reflects a deep property of a \emph{geometrically stable waveguide}: when
        the waveguide structure (bathymetry, SVP) is held fixed, a frequency change
        shifts all multipath interference fringes \emph{coherently} across the
        range--depth plane.
        Every pixel experiences the same fractional TL variation; the spatial pattern of
        propagation is preserved --- only its phase advances.
        Frequency is a tuning parameter that modulates amplitude without reorganising
        the spatial structure.
        Contrast with SVP, which physically reshapes the ray geometry: changing SVP
        moves caustics, alters critical angles, and reorganises which modes propagate ---
        a qualitatively different, spatially non-uniform effect.

\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Operational translation.}
The asymmetry between SVP-driven heterogeneous variance and frequency-driven uniform variance
has a direct mission-planning implication:
\emph{a pre-mission bathythermograph (BT) drop to measure the sound-speed profile
delivers far more reduction in acoustic uncertainty than any frequency-calibration
measurement.}
The 8--10\,dB acoustic uncertainty widely cited in the sonar engineering literature
arises primarily from SVP mismatch, not from signal-processing or platform calibration.
This is consistent with the NATO operational experience that SVP measurement quality
is the single strongest predictor of sonar performance prediction accuracy.

% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{What PCE Non-Convergence Reveals About the Waveguide}
\label{sec:pce_physics}

Polynomial Chaos Expansion converges exponentially for \emph{analytic} (infinitely
differentiable) functions of the uncertain parameter $\xi$.
For flat-bottom deep water with SVP perturbation, our $K^* = 4$--$6$ results confirm
that $\mathrm{TL}(\xi_\text{svp})$ is smooth: the acoustic field changes continuously
and slowly as SVP varies, and the polynomial series converges.
This is physically sensible --- in an isovelocity waveguide perturbed by a small Gaussian
SVP fluctuation, the ray paths bend smoothly and caustic positions shift continuously.

For \emph{slope environments}, none of the three parameters converge at any order
$K \leq 20$.
This is a statement about the mathematical regularity of $\mathrm{TL}(\xi)$ in those
environments:
the TL field, viewed as a function of the uncertain parameter, has finite-order singularities
corresponding to physical caustic crossings.
As the bathymetric slope changes mode-coupling conditions, small parameter changes
can create or annihilate a caustic crossing at a fixed observation point.
At such a crossing, the acoustic pressure transitions between an Airy-function regime
(near caustic) and a shadow-zone regime --- a non-smooth change in parameter space that
no finite polynomial order can represent.

The PCE $K^*$ map therefore functions as a \emph{waveguide regularity diagnostic}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $K^* \leq 6$: the waveguide response to this parameter is smooth and polynomial
        at this perturbation level --- lightweight surrogates are reliable.
  \item $K^* = 7$--$10$: moderate nonlinearity; PCE is usable with cross-validation,
        but with acknowledged variance bias.
  \item $K^* > 10$ or non-convergence: the waveguide response is genuinely
        non-polynomial for this parameter; surrogates should not be trusted and MC is
        mandatory.
\end{itemize}

Crucially, a sonar operator or planning tool that applies a well-calibrated flat-water
surrogate to a slope environment will report \emph{confidently wrong} low-variance
estimates, because the surrogate was fitted on a smooth TL surface and extrapolates
that smoothness into a nonlinear regime.
The automated LOO-CV convergence diagnostic flags this failure mode: any scenario
with LOO error $> 10\%$ at all orders signals ``surrogate unreliable; fall back to MC.''

% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{The Three Predictability Regimes}
\label{sec:three_regimes}

Synthesising the results across all 36 parameter--scenario combinations, the ocean
acoustic environment partitions into three predictability regimes:

\begin{enumerate}

  \item \textbf{High-predictability} (flat bathymetry, $\sigma \leq 1\%$):
        $K^* = 1$--$4$, Delta method accurate, LN3 fits well.
        Sonar models can be trusted; prediction errors are $\lesssim$\,5\,dB standard
        deviation.
        All five UQ methods agree; the cheapest (Delta) is sufficient.

  \item \textbf{Moderate-predictability} (flat bathymetry, $\sigma = 5$--$10\%$):
        $K^* = 4$--$9$ for SVP and frequency, $K^* > 10$ for $z_S$.
        PCE converges for two of the three parameters; MC provides the gold standard.
        Prediction errors reach 8--10\,dB for SVP and $z_S$, consistent with the
        standard acoustic uncertainty figure.
        Mission planning should include $\pm\sigma$ confidence intervals on detection range.

  \item \textbf{Low-predictability} (slope bathymetry, any $\sigma$; or
        $z_S$ perturbation at any magnitude):
        No polynomial surrogate converges; Delta fails ($>$\,96\% second-order correction
        fraction); LN3 fails at bimodal shadow-zone pixels.
        Only MC with a large sample count is reliable, and the Chebyshev bound
        (Section~\ref{sec:pdetect_compare}) provides the only distribution-free safety
        margin.
        Dual-hypothesis detection strategies are required: the operator should plan
        simultaneously for ``CZ present at predicted range'' and ``CZ shifted or absent,''
        assigning probability weights from the MC $P(\mathrm{TL} > \mathrm{FOM})$ maps.

\end{enumerate}

The boundaries between these regimes are \emph{not global} --- they depend on the local
acoustic environment and cannot be pre-determined without running the propagation model.
This is precisely what the stochastic Bellhop pipeline provides: a spatial metadata layer
that tells the operator not just ``where is TL high or low'' but ``where can we trust our
predictions.''

% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Practical Guidance for Sonar Operators}
\label{sec:operator_guidance}

\paragraph{1. The confidence map matters more than the mean map.}
A deterministic TL map shows where mean predicted loss is high (shadow zones) and low
(convergence zones).
But the operationally critical quantity is the probability that TL exceeds the FOM ---
not whether the mean TL does.
Our $P(\mathrm{TL} > \mathrm{FOM})$ maps reveal a key asymmetry: convergence zones are
simultaneously high-gain (low mean TL) \emph{and} high-variance (TL varies by $\pm$20--30\,dB
across environmental realisations).
A sonar operator who relies on convergence-zone gain for detection will succeed most of the
time but miss a significant fraction of contacts --- not because the target is absent, but
because the acoustic environment failed to focus energy as predicted.
CZ position can shift by $\pm$10\,km and CZ gain can vary by $\pm$5--10\,dB under realistic
SVP uncertainty; this variability is captured in the MC-derived confidence maps but invisible
in any deterministic TL prediction.

\paragraph{2. Use the P95 TL envelope for procurement and platform design.}
For conservative sonar planning (NATO-style ``worst credible case'' requirement),
the P95 TL map $\mathrm{TL}^{95}(r,z) = \mathrm{Q}_{0.95}[\mathrm{TL}]$ is the
appropriate design baseline:
a sonar system whose FOM exceeds $\mathrm{TL}^{95}$ will detect targets in 95\% of
environmental realisations within its specifications.
For a Normal TL distribution, $\mathrm{TL}^{95} \approx \mu + 1.645\,\sigma$, which is
recoverable from the Delta or PCE variance at zero extra Bellhop cost.
Our analysis confirms this Normal approximation holds at $\sim$75\% of pixels, with the
remainder at shadow-zone boundaries where the distribution is skewed or bimodal.

\paragraph{3. Flag slope-environment contacts at elevated uncertainty.}
The complete failure of lightweight surrogates in upslope and downslope geometries is not
a modelling inconvenience --- it is a warning that acoustic prediction in these environments
is fundamentally harder.
A contact in a flat-water convergence zone has high detection confidence; the same sonar
signature in a slope environment carries lower detection confidence even at identical
measured SNR, because the TL model underlying the detection hypothesis is itself unreliable.
Operationally, acoustic contacts acquired in slope regions should be flagged with an
elevated uncertainty tier, triggering closer-range confirmation or multi-sensor correlation
before commitment.

\paragraph{4. Source depth uncertainty is a new and under-appreciated risk.}
The $z_S$-PCE non-convergence in all Normal distribution cases ($K^* > 10$) has no
published counterpart and points to a physically intuitive but operationally neglected risk:
when the adversary's depth is unknown, the TL model loses accuracy
\emph{even in flat-water geometries where SVP is well characterised}.
Fleet-vs.-fleet detection planning that does not include a source-depth uncertainty term
in the acoustic error budget is systematically over-confident.
The MC variance maps for $z_S$ perturbation provide a first estimate of this budget; the
non-polynomial nature of the sensitivity surface means the only safe approach is
large-ensemble MC, not linearised sensitivity analysis.

\paragraph{5. Match the UQ method to the operating regime.}
Table~\ref{tab:method_tradeoff} distils the regime-specific method recommendation,
but the deeper principle is this:
\emph{the appropriate level of computational investment is determined by the waveguide's
local sensitivity structure, not by computational convenience.}
In high-predictability regions, the cheapest method (Delta, three extra Bellhop runs) is
accurate.
In low-predictability regions, the costliest method (MC, 1000+ runs) is the only reliable
one.
The stochastic pipeline's variance maps make this structure visible before the operator
commits to a method, enabling adaptive budget allocation:
spend simulation resources where the waveguide is hard, not uniformly across the domain.

\subsection{Spatial Accuracy of Variance Estimation Across the Acoustic Map}
\label{sec:var_accuracy}

The script \texttt{compare\_variance\_spatial.m} quantifies pixel-wise relative variance error
\begin{equation}
  \varepsilon(r,z) \;=\; \frac{|\hat V_\text{method}(r,z) - \hat V_\text{MC}(r,z)|}%
                               {\hat V_\text{MC}(r,z) + 10^{-10}}
  \label{eq:rel_var_err}
\end{equation}
(capped at~$2.0$ to prevent blow-up at near-zero MC variance pixels) for each of the three
approximation methods --- Delta, LN3, and PCE at $K^*$ --- compared against the Monte Carlo
reference $\hat V_\text{MC}$.
Dark pixels in the heat-maps indicate accurate variance estimation; bright/red pixels indicate
large relative error.
This diagnostic answers the operationally critical question: \emph{where in the range--depth
plane can each surrogate method be trusted?}

\subsubsection{Near-Source / Direct-Path Region (0--10\,km)}

Close to the source, propagation is dominated by the geometric spreading of the direct path
and the first surface reflection.
In this regime $\mathrm{TL}(r,z)$ is nearly linear in all three uncertain parameters, so:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Delta} achieves its lowest relative error here --- typically $< 10\%$ for
        flat scenarios at $\sigma \leq 5\%$.
        The 1st-order Taylor approximation is valid because the acoustic field changes smoothly
        with each parameter.
  \item \textbf{LN3} performs equally well: the TL distribution in the direct-path region is
        nearly Gaussian (few multipath contributions), so the lognormal model is a good fit.
  \item \textbf{PCE at $K^* = 1$--$2$} converges quickly, consistent with near-linear TL
        dependence.
\end{itemize}
\emph{Practical implication}: for short-range detection problems (within the first Fresnel
zone, $r < 10$\,km), the Delta method is computationally sufficient and introduces
negligible variance error.

\subsubsection{Convergence Zones}

At convergence-zone (CZ) arcs ($r \approx 55$--$65$\,km in deep water), ray caustics focus
energy into narrow bands.
Small changes in SVP, frequency, or source depth shift the caustic position by several
kilometres, causing 10--20\,dB TL swings at CZ-boundary pixels.
This strong nonlinearity degrades all three approximation methods relative to MC:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Delta}: systematically underestimates variance at the CZ arc (the linear
        approximation cannot capture the caustic transition) while overestimating in the
        geometrically stable insonified region.
        Relative error exceeds $100\%$ at CZ boundaries for $\sigma \geq 5\%$.
  \item \textbf{LN3}: the shifted-lognormal model is imprecise near CZ boundaries because the
        TL distribution there is bimodal (mixing CZ-insonified and CZ-shadow realisations
        from different environmental draws).
        Errors of $50$--$150\%$ are typical at the caustic arc.
  \item \textbf{PCE at $K^*$}: performs best at CZ boundaries when $K^* \geq 5$, because the
        higher-order polynomial terms capture the curvature of $\mathrm{TL}(\xi)$ near the
        caustic.
        Residual error at CZ edges is typically $20$--$60\%$ --- significantly below Delta and
        LN3 --- but non-negligible.
\end{itemize}
\emph{Practical implication}: no lightweight surrogate reliably predicts variance at CZ
boundaries.
For mission planning in the CZ, PCE with $K^* \geq 5$ provides the best surrogate approximation,
but a full MC ensemble remains the gold standard.

\subsubsection{Shadow Zones}

Shadow zones are geometrically excluded from direct-path and refracted energy: all realisations
agree that the field is attenuated, so the variance is intrinsically low.
The relative error metric is therefore misleading in the shadow core
(small numerator \emph{and} small denominator).
All three methods correctly identify the low-variance character of the shadow core,
with $\varepsilon < 30\%$.
Errors concentrate instead at the shadow-zone \emph{boundary} (the penumbra), which
is precisely the CZ-edge problem described above.
In slope environments (upslope, downslope), the penumbra boundary itself is uncertain ---
it shifts with each realisation --- making the shadow boundary an inherently high-error region
for any fixed-order surrogate.

\subsubsection{Far Field Beyond the First CZ (60--150\,km)}

Beyond the first CZ, the acoustic field is shaped by the second and third convergence zones.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Delta} errors grow monotonically with range: each additional CZ arc introduces
        new nonlinearity that the 1st-order approximation cannot absorb.
        By $r > 80$\,km, Delta errors routinely exceed $200\%$ for Normal\,10\% distributions.
  \item \textbf{PCE at $K^* \geq 6$} maintains near-MC accuracy out to the second CZ
        ($r \approx 110$--$120$\,km) for SVP and frequency perturbations.
        For source-depth perturbations, PCE degrades beyond the first CZ even at high $K^*$,
        consistent with the source-depth PCE non-convergence discussed in
        Section~\ref{sec:pce_physics}.
  \item \textbf{LN3}: intermediate performance.
        The shifted-lognormal captures first-order skewness in the TL distribution but fails
        at pixels where two separate convergence-zone modes contribute, producing a bimodal
        TL distribution that the unimodal LN3 cannot represent.
\end{itemize}
\emph{Practical implication}: for extended-range operations beyond the second CZ, only MC
and PCE (for SVP/frequency, not $z_S$) are reliable variance estimators.

\subsubsection{Slope Environments}

In upslope and downslope scenarios the bathymetric gradient continuously relocates the CZ
position as range increases.
The shadow boundary is fundamentally range-dependent, varying realisation by realisation.
Variance-error results for slope environments:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{All three surrogates} exhibit errors $> 100\%$ across the mid-range shadow
        region, where the boundary position is most uncertain.
  \item \textbf{PCE} shows tolerable accuracy ($< 40\%$) only in the far-range fully-insonified
        region ($r > 40$\,km in the downslope scenario), where the field is no longer sensitive
        to shadow-boundary location.
  \item \textbf{Delta} fails throughout slope geometries, confirming the conclusion of
        Section~\ref{sec:delta_synthesis}.
  \item \textbf{LN3} partially captures the marginal variance but misrepresents the spatial
        correlation structure --- a problem not visible in pixel-wise RMSE but critical for
        joint probability calculations.
\end{itemize}
\emph{Practical implication}: slope environments require full MC for reliable variance maps.
The var-accuracy heat-maps provide a run-time diagnostic: pixels where $\varepsilon > 50\%$
should be flagged as ``MC required'' in any automated planning tool.
